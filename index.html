PROBE A · Developed from Probe 2

Make with AI on My Cuff or Bag

Explore a source pattern on a cuff or bag, respond to the AI preview with a material action, then compare what changes.

Material action & authenticityWorking concept under development
A

The research question

How do people maintain or revise their understandings of authenticity through interaction with AI and cultural heritage?

A explores how material actions and AI image responses affect a person’s understanding of authenticity. A named textile object supplies the source. The cuff or bag is one everyday way to encounter its patterns.

AI’s intended role

In the working concept, AI adapts a named source pattern to an approved local cuff or bag photograph. The person’s next fold or position change becomes the next input. The source, photograph and AI preview remain visible together.

WORKING PROBE CONCEPT
Approve a photo→See AI’s preview→Fold or reposition→Compare the next response

Round 1 explores this direction through low-fidelity prototypes.

See its Round 1 version →

The intended interaction

  1. Choose a source and item

    Read the textile object’s information and select a detail. Choose a cuff or bag area, with a study item available.

  2. Approve a small photograph

    Place the chosen area in the fixed tabletop window. Check and approve the local photograph.

  3. See AI’s first preview

    AI combines the source detail and approved photograph to propose a digital pattern preview.

  4. Make a physical response

    Fold, flatten or reposition the material after seeing the preview. Approve another local photograph, or keep the current result.

  5. Compare and explain

    Compare the two actual previews. Point to a change and explain what it carries from the source or your own experience.

  6. Reflect and keep the result

    Discuss the clearly labelled future museum arrangement and keep a source-credited visual record.

The working A concept

A person’s material action guides AI’s next visual response. Place one cuff or bag area in a small fixed camera window. Keep the named source, approved local photograph and AI preview visible together. A study item or prepared input offers another way to join.

  1. See: compare the source with the first digital preview.
  2. Act: fold or reposition the plain material.
  3. Explain: compare the response and say what keeps a connection to the source.

How authenticity enters A

Ask what the person keeps or changes in their understanding of authenticity as the source pattern enters a new material and personal setting. They may draw on resemblance, a maker’s history, a material relationship or something from their own life. Treat liking, control and authenticity as separate judgments. [1] [2]

The connection to Art Morph

Art Morph already links physical materials, a camera and generated images. A builds its research focus around a named heritage source and people’s reasons for maintaining or revising what feels authentic. The potential contribution is an account of that process, developed through iterative research. Body Prompting and A(I)Cam provide further embodied-generation precedents. [22] [29] [30] [31]

Theory, HCI and possible contribution

Connections between people and objects

Relational authenticity asks how a connection becomes meaningful through objects, people, making and history. We follow the connections visitors themselves describe. [1]

Looking alike and having a real link

A similar shape is one possible reason. A known maker, source or history gives another kind of link. The personal item and original work each retain their own materials and histories. [2]

Action and response

We examine whether a visitor can see how their material action affects AI, guide the next image and decide when the result no longer fits the source. Embodied generation already has published precedents. [22] [29] [30]

What the research could contribute

The candidate contribution is an account of how visitors maintain or change their grounds for a connection to one named heritage object during material interaction with AI. The interaction technique has close precedents. A contribution would need evidence from the action sequence and the visitor’s reasons. [1] [2] [6] [7] [12] [22] [29] [30]

Evidence to develop across iterations

For a later working-prototype study, a proposed record could link the source, approved photographs, AI previews, physical adjustments and participant explanations. The collection method would be specified in that study’s protocol. Round 1 uses the smaller, separate data set described below.

The working-prototype research would follow how people connect their actions, AI responses, the source work and their own experience. Round 1 first gathers written authenticity reflections, optional captions and source/image choices.

Use Round 1 feedback to refine the task, examples and research prompts. Later iterations can test the live action-and-response loop and choose a detailed study method.

PROBE B · Combines Probes 4 and 5

One Object, Two Interpretations

Exchange interpretations of the same object across languages, clarify the meaning, and develop a postcard that carries the exchange forward.

Cross-language exchangeWorking concept under development
B

The research question

How do people express and negotiate their understanding of a shared heritage object through AI-mediated cross-cultural exchange?

B explores expression and negotiation across cultural perspectives. The shared object anchors the conversation. AI offers language and image assistance, while original messages, real replies and clarifications remain visible. A related postcard develops from the exchange.

AI’s intended role

In the working concept, AI bridges languages and helps visualise an interpretation. It can assist a translation or image revision after a real response. People check their own expression and retain the original messages and conversation.

WORKING PROBE CONCEPT
Express a meaning→AI assists→Receive a reply→Clarify and keep a card

Round 1 explores this direction through low-fidelity prototypes.

See its Round 1 version →

The intended interaction

  1. Share one named object

    Use the same object image, selected detail and checked information. An approved cuff or bag photograph can provide a personal visual setting.

  2. Express an interpretation

    Write or say what the object means to you in your own language. Check the original words.

  3. Develop and share a draft

    AI assists with translation and a source-related image preview. Original words, labelled translation and source remain visible.

  4. Receive a real response

    Another person reads the source and interpretation, then replies, asks a question or offers a different reading.

  5. Clarify and update

    Keep the conversation visible. Clarify the words and, when useful, revise the associated image and postcard. Different interpretations can remain.

  6. Keep a connected postcard

    Choose the postcard linked to the exchange. The concept includes download or optional email delivery, with the delivery method to be specified for a later study.

The working B concept

AI helps people express and negotiate interpretations of the same object across languages and cultural perspectives. Original messages, real responses and clarifications remain visible. A related visual postcard carries a selected interpretation from the exchange.

  1. Express: keep the original message beside the AI-assisted translation.
  2. Reply: another person asks or offers a different interpretation.
  3. Carry forward: clarify the meaning and update its related postcard.

A postcard grows from the exchange

Use one selected message as the link between conversation and postcard. AI can assist a translation or image preview. After another person responds, the author can clarify the meaning and revise the associated card. Original words, translations and source information remain identified.

What B investigates

B focuses on cross-cultural expression, understanding and negotiation. A person may recognise their own voice, encounter a different interpretation or clarify a detail. Relational authenticity and work on personal voice help distinguish these experiences from historical accuracy and translation fluency. [1] [3] [8] [9] [17]

Keeping the outcome

The working concept includes a personal postcard people can retain after the exchange. Download or optional email delivery can be developed with an appropriate later procedure. Round 1 offers paper copies of participants’ own cards and collects no contacts. [15] [26]

First prototype round: see the local conversation and prepared-image postcard used in Round 1 →

Theory, HCI and possible contribution

Relational authenticity

Follow how the object, a person’s experience and the other visitor become connected through exchange. [1] [16]

Personal voice and recognition

Separate the sender’s endorsement of wording, the recipient’s understanding, historical accuracy and the recognition of an interpretation. [3] [17]

Agency and cultural expression

Being able to change a translation can help people express themselves. It can also make a conversation take more effort. AI writing suggestions may remove cultural details. These findings help us choose what to observe. [8] [9] [10]

What the research could contribute

The candidate contribution is an account of how a source-related personal meaning develops through a multilingual conversation around one AI-assisted visual postcard. Its draft and revision show how people recognise, question and clarify each other’s interpretations. The final card keeps the outcome of that same process. [1] [3] [8] [9] [10] [16] [17] [25] [26]

Evidence to develop across iterations

A later working-prototype study could link original messages, translations, real replies, clarifications and related postcard versions. Approved local photographs and delivery choices would need their own specified handling. Round 1 collects the anonymous text and existing-image references listed in its protocol.

The working concept asks how meanings travel through expression, AI assistance, another person’s reply and clarification. Round 1 uses original messages, reply relationships, related postcards and questionnaire reflections to refine that activity.

Use Round 1 to improve the conversation and postcard relationship. Later iterations can test live language and image assistance and specify the study procedure.

01 · Ask About a Detail

Choose part of an object image. Say what you notice. Ask AI one question, then say what you think after its answer.

Possible concept evidence: the chosen detail, question, actual answer and any change in the visitor’s explanation.

After an AI answer, what does a visitor use to decide what to believe about an object?

We look for reasons, such as a visible detail, a museum source or personal knowledge. We also ask when the person checks or rejects the answer.

A connection to an object can come from its history, appearance or a personal memory. These are different reasons. We keep them separate when reading the visitor’s account. This uses relational authenticity and the difference between looking alike and having a real source link. [1] [2]The book case. The Statistical Clock

The Statistical Clock is an imagined household clock that reports deaths in different kinds of transport. It takes information people may avoid and puts it into an ordinary daily object. The designers ask what kind of life would make someone want this clock. The clock helps people discuss that life.

The idea we borrow. Put a familiar rule into a small, visible object so people can discuss how that rule shapes daily life.

How it connects to this probe. The clock makes daily reminders visible. Probe 1 can make the rule “AI speaks first” visible through a small museum display card. The question then concerns how that rule shapes the start of a visit.

The step it changes. After the normal question task, place the AI answer at the top of a clearly imagined museum display card. Put the visitor’s own explanation beside it. Ask what changes when the AI version always goes first.

How this helps us choose. Keep 1 as a short introduction. A future standalone version could focus on who sets the starting point of interpretation.

03 · Change One Detail

Select an area of an image, tell AI what may change, then compare the changed version with the source.

Possible concept evidence: selected area, instruction, actual image and reasons for keeping or rejecting it.

Which changes still feel connected to the object, and which changes lose that connection?

We need to hear why a change matters. Ask what the preferred picture carries from the source. The HCI issue is how people set and check limits for generation.

The difference between resemblance and a real source link helps us separate “looks like it” from “still carries something important from it”. We also record personal preference on its own. [2] [5]The book case. Plant Fiction

Troika’s Plant Fiction presents invented plants with plausible images and short texts about how they might be used in a near-future London. The plants belong to a made-up situation. The project tells viewers that it is fiction, so they can explore the situation without being told a false discovery has occurred. One example, Selfeater, is imagined to break down its own material to help make fuel. Its picture and short story let viewers think about that future use.

The idea we borrow. Give an imagined image a clear setting and purpose, so viewers can explore the proposed future.

How it connects to this probe. Plant Fiction explains what its invented plants would do in future London. Probe 3 needs an equally clear purpose for its changed pattern. Adapting one detail to a cuff or bag gives the change a concrete everyday use.

The step it changes. Show the source, one local-item adaptation and a short label describing its imagined use. Ask which source detail the person wants to carry into that use and why.

How this helps us choose. Keep 3 as a possible focused variant. A already uses a local item and visual changes. A separate Probe 3 would need its own use or research question.

The authors’ words.

There is a very important difference between inviting viewers to “make-believe” and asking them to “believe.”

01 / MAKING

Huayao embroidery: follow a change and its reason

The original study

Eight experienced Huayao embroiderers worked through a six-month, four-stage process. It included 152 embroidery images, three generation workflows, selecting images and making embroidery. The final in-situ stages lasted eight days. Researchers used interviews during making, a focus group and open coding. The publication is a CHI 2026 extended abstract. [12]

What the researchers observed

Participants selected, discarded and changed generated images. Their reasons included familiar local motifs, animal forms, line density and what would work in embroidery. The authors describe how making helped bring further judgments into view.

Dianhua: “I still have to change it a bit”. The authors’ discussion: “many of the modifications made during the making offer insights”. [12]

The step this changes in A

Connect the source, example and participant’s written reason clearly. In the A concept, follow the source, physical adjustment, AI response and the person’s reason together. Round 1 begins with material arrangement and prepared effects. Its questionnaire and optional caption help refine how to explore authenticity. Experienced embroidery and a brief visitor activity offer different settings for this way of examining making.

The study informs our attention to reasons that develop through practice. A’s visitor task investigates how those reasons relate a named work to a personal item. See the authenticity guide and the Art Morph comparison. [1] [2] [12]

02 / SOCIAL RELATIONSHIPS

The AI Pattern Merchant: place a working design in a social setting

What happened

The project brought prompting, generation and printing into a Huayao festival and the relationships of making and exchange. The authors report attention to the inserted samples and record that none were purchased. [11]

The design lesson

A working object gives people something concrete to encounter and discuss. A’s intended previews and B’s conversation-linked postcard make a wider museum arrangement imaginable. Round 1 begins with prepared examples and a local whiteboard.

03 / LANGUAGE

Let people clarify in their own language

What the study found

A study of 45 dyads of Chinese new immigrants and English-speaking residents in the US examined translation interfaces. Post-editing supported greater exercise of agency and added costs to the conversation. [9]

The step this changes in B

B’s concept uses AI language assistance while preserving original words and real replies. Round 1 explores the conversation and its related postcard through original-language messages, prepared images and labelled multilingual starter notes. Participant text stays outside AI services in this round.

Research on cultural detail in AI writing and colour-name translation also helps us look closely at what a fluent version carries. [8] [10]

Useful museum and HCI precedents

Cleveland Museum of Art

Art Morph connects materials with generated art. Endless Threads combines a camera cutout with a chosen textile using OpenCV. These offer two forms for linking a person with a collection image. [22] [29]

GIFT and personal records

GIFT connects objects with personal messages. Museum data souvenirs connect a visit with a personal take-away. B develops that take-away inside the conversation. [15] [16] [26]

Embodied generation

Body-prompting research and A(I)Cam show ways to make generation an embodied public or reflective experience. A borrows the idea of material exploration. Its Round 1 prototypes use prepared everyday-item effects and written reflections. [30] [31]

Other research and reading scope

AI co-writing informs the distinction between a writer’s approval and a reader’s understanding. Heritage narratives, TwistLens and Gen-Diaolou add context for interpretation and transformation. Nasher provides an AI curatorial-text precedent. [3] [4] [5] [14] [25]

ZOMA is used through its abstract as a hybrid-craft precedent. The empathy paper is also used at abstract level as a prompt to consider attribution. Source entries identify the material read and the study setting. [7] [20]

06 / SPECULATIVE DESIGN REASONING

The original words and the ideas they give us

The book cases explain the working concepts and their imagined museum arrangements. Round 1 has its own limited procedure and questions.

A possible future for A

A museum shares adaptations for everyday cuffs and bags. A small postcard can help people imagine how a source pattern enters personal life.

A possible future for B

A museum shows visitor interpretations together with the replies that shaped them. A postcard wall makes questions about visibility and voice concrete.

How this informs the two concepts. Use clear source-to-example links in A and keep original words with the chosen image in B. The concepts use these props to open discussion about museum interpretation. Round 1 explores their initial forms through the submitted activities and questionnaire. Further iterations can develop the speculative encounter.

Thirteen original passages from Anthony Dunne and Fiona Raby’s Speculative Everything (2013) are preserved below with printed page numbers. Interpretations and design applications are ours. Book figures keep their original captions and credits. [21]

Passage 1 · pp. 2–3Use possible futures to understand the present

What we are interested in, though, is the idea of possible futures and using them as tools to better understand the present and to discuss the kind of future people want, and, of course, ones people do not want.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 1, pp. 2–3. [21]

The idea we take from it

A possible future helps people examine present choices and discuss how they want to live.

How it informs the design

A imagines a museum work continuing through personal-item patterns. B imagines a museum displaying postcards that grow through conversations. The possible futures help visitors examine their own interaction.

Passage 2 · pp. 3A future is a medium for thinking

For us futures are not a destination or something to be strived for but a medium to aid imaginative thought—to speculate with.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 1, pp. 3. [21]

The idea we take from it

A possible future lets people think about a way of living and decide what they want to keep or change.

How it informs the design

After the ordinary activity, introduce a future museum arrangement. Ask what it would change and what visitors would keep, alter or refuse. This discussion connects the small AI task to choices about museum life.

Passage 3 · pp. 90A prop makes another possibility discussable

They help us think about alternative possibilities—they challenge the ideals, values, and beliefs of our society embodied in material culture.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 6, pp. 90. [21]

The idea we take from it

The object supports reflection on the values embodied in a possible arrangement.

How it informs the design

A uses its local-item previews and a future label. B uses the draft, reply and final postcard. Each small prop gives people something concrete to point to when they discuss a museum’s choices.

Passage 4 · pp. 92A prop can actually work

For some, the term prop means a fake object, something that doesn’t work, but in the context of a speculative design, a prop can be a fully working prototype or not.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 6, pp. 92. [21]

The idea we take from it

A working prototype can serve as a prop for an imagined situation. Its role is to make that situation available for discussion.

How it informs the design

Run the actual image and translation functions during the study. Use their outputs as props for the later, clearly introduced future scenario.

Passage 5 · pp. 93Leave space for people’s own interpretations

Prefigurative futures (or pre-enactments?) also use props to transport viewers’ imagination but, whereas in this role they are bringing a specific future to the present so that it can be partially experienced in advance, user-tested, or prepared for, we are more interested in using props to transport viewers’ imagination into a thought experiment, or what-if, and allow enough room for them to make their own interpretations.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 6, pp. 93. [21]

The idea we take from it

Participants can give their own answers to an imagined situation. The researcher should leave those answers open.

How it informs the design

Visitors can accept, alter or stop an AI preview. They can keep different interpretations after understanding one another. Their choices shape the discussion.

Passage 6 · pp. 161People may make their own judgments

We view people as free agents, not necessarily rational, but free to make up their own minds.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 9, pp. 161. [21]

The idea we take from it

This is a commitment to participants’ freedom to judge and choose.

How it informs the design

People can choose the AI route, a prepared input, a plain card and their receipt method. Pilot sessions reveal which routes need further support.

Passage 7 · pp. 2Create a space for discussion

This form of design thrives on imagination and aims to open up new perspectives on what are sometimes called wicked problems, to create spaces for discussion and debate about alternative ways of being, and to inspire and encourage people’s imaginations to flow freely.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 1, pp. 2. [21]

The idea we take from it

Speculation opens a discussion about how life could be different. A feature test needs this wider question to become part of that discussion.

How it informs the design

A discusses how personal use carries a work forward. B discusses how a museum can show a meaning that developed between people. Both invite alternative answers.

Passage 8 · pp. 35An alternative must open a gap with the present

It is the gap between reality as we know it and the different idea of reality referred to in the critical design proposal that creates the space for discussion.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 3, pp. 35. [21]

The idea we take from it

A proposal opens discussion by making another arrangement available. Its fictional rule gives participants a concrete alternative to compare with their present experience.

How it informs the design

For each probe, explain the current activity, the future museum rule, the prop that makes it visible and the choice left to participants. This sequence explains why we select and revise a direction.

Passage 9 · pp. 35–36The Statistical Clock · reasoning for Probe 01

Dunne & Raby with Michael Anastassiades. The Statistical Clock is an imagined household clock that reports deaths in different kinds of transport. It takes information people may avoid and puts it into an ordinary daily object. The designers ask what kind of life would make someone want this clock. The clock helps people discuss that life.

What would have to change for a need like this to emerge?

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 3, pp. 35–36. [21]

The idea we take from it

Put a familiar rule into a small, visible object so people can discuss how that rule shapes daily life. The clock makes daily reminders visible. Probe 1 can make the rule “AI speaks first” visible through a small museum display card. The question then concerns how that rule shapes the start of a visit.

How it informs the design

After the normal question task, place the AI answer at the top of a clearly imagined museum display card. Put the visitor’s own explanation beside it. Ask what changes when the AI version always goes first. Keep 1 as a short introduction. A future standalone version could focus on who sets the starting point of interpretation.

Passage 10 · pp. 92–93The Islanders · reasoning for Probe 02

Charles Avery, discussed by Dunne & Raby. In Charles Avery’s The Islanders, drawings, objects, models and found bones appear to come from a traveller’s visit to an invented island. Viewers use these small clues to imagine a larger place and its way of life. A small physical thing can therefore help someone think about an imagined world.

It is the emphasis on transporting the imagination that distinguishes them from other object types including products, prototypes, and models.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 6, pp. 92–93. [21]

The idea we take from it

A small object can help people imagine the larger world in which it belongs. The island’s objects make an invented place imaginable. A uses the cuff or bag preview and a future museum label to suggest a museum where heritage patterns keep entering everyday belongings.

How it informs the design

Complete A’s two local captures. Show the two actual previews beside a fictional label saying “Each version continues the original work”. Ask what kind of connection the wearable or everyday version carries forward. Develop A around the local material action and its two visual responses. The future label helps visitors connect their experience with a wider question about how a museum work can continue through personal use.

Passage 11 · pp. 94–95Plant Fiction · reasoning for Probe 03

Troika, discussed by Dunne & Raby. Troika’s Plant Fiction presents invented plants with plausible images and short texts about how they might be used in a near-future London. The plants belong to a made-up situation. The project tells viewers that it is fiction, so they can explore the situation without being told a false discovery has occurred. One example, Selfeater, is imagined to break down its own material to help make fuel. Its picture and short story let viewers think about that future use.

There is a very important difference between inviting viewers to “make-believe” and asking them to “believe.”

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 6, pp. 94–95. [21]

The idea we take from it

Give an imagined image a clear setting and purpose, so viewers can explore the proposed future. Plant Fiction explains what its invented plants would do in future London. Probe 3 needs an equally clear purpose for its changed pattern. Adapting one detail to a cuff or bag gives the change a concrete everyday use.

How it informs the design

Show the source, one local-item adaptation and a short label describing its imagined use. Ask which source detail the person wants to carry into that use and why. Keep 3 as a possible focused variant. A already uses a local item and visual changes. A separate Probe 3 would need its own use or research question.

Passage 12 · pp. 173–174United Micro Kingdoms · reasoning for Probe 04

Dunne & Raby. United Micro Kingdoms imagines several different societies and designs a vehicle for each one. Each vehicle shows choices about how people travel and live together. Looking at them helps viewers compare what each society makes easier and what it gives up. The pictured Digicars belong to a made-up society where software manages travel and road prices. The Biocar belongs to a society built around biotechnology. These two images show how different choices can shape everyday travel.

Each vehicle would embody different ideologies, values, priorities, and belief systems—essentially alternative worldviews.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 9, pp. 173–174. [21]

The idea we take from it

Make different choices visible through objects people can compare. The vehicles show different ways of organising travel and everyday life. B’s first draft, reply and final postcard show choices about whose interpretation becomes visible. The same card makes those choices easy to point to.

How it informs the design

Keep the draft postcard, the real reply and the final postcard together. After the exchange, use a fictional museum display label and ask whether visitors should see the final postcard alone or the conversation that shaped it. Combine 4 and 5 as B. Keep the conversation visible and use its related postcard as a concrete prop for discussing interpretation and display. Round 1 begins with a local message area and prepared-image cards.

Passage 13 · pp. 40–42Huggable Atomic Mushrooms · reasoning for Probe 05

Dunne & Raby with Michael Anastassiades. Huggable Atomic Mushrooms are soft objects shaped like clouds from nuclear tests. Their friendly feel sits beside a frightening subject. This makes viewers ask why an inviting object presents that subject in this way. Their meaning comes from this relationship between form and subject.

It is through its demeanor that one starts to wonder just how serious it is.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 3, pp. 40–42. [21]

The idea we take from it

The way an object looks and feels can change how its subject is understood. The soft mushroom form changes how viewers meet the subject of nuclear tests. In B, a friendly pattern preview can also change the tone of a personal account. The recipient’s real response shows what the draft conveyed.

How it informs the design

Let the recipient respond to the first postcard, including its image. The sender then keeps or clarifies its words and picture on the same card. Ask which version expresses the intended connection to the object. Combine 4 and 5 as B. Keep the conversation visible and use its related postcard as a concrete prop for discussing interpretation and display. Round 1 begins with a local message area and prepared-image cards.
