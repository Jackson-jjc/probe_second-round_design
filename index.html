What feels authentic
when AI joins the visit?

Two short activities explore how people connect with heritage objects, express their own experience and understand another visitor.

A probe gives someone something to try and talk about. Here, “authenticity” is an open question about a real or meaningful connection with an object, its history or other people. We ask visitors what that connection rests on.

The wider research question and what “authenticity” means here

How do human–AI interactions shape visitors’ experiences of authenticity in museums and heritage sites?

The two supporting questions ask what visitors draw on for authenticity, and how they negotiate different senses of authenticity when interpreting heritage objects with AI. A and B are focused parts of this larger project.

We do not treat historical accuracy, resemblance, felt connection, personal voice and another person’s understanding as interchangeable. Their relationships are what the activities help us examine. The theories help us decide what to notice. Visitors can give their own reasons and feelings. [1] [2] [3] [17]

01 / THE TWO CURRENT DIRECTIONS

Two ways into the same research problem

Choose A to move materials and guide AI images. Choose B to exchange meanings with another person and make an optional postcard. Visitors take part in one short activity, not a five-part programme.

DIRECTION A · Developed from Probe 2

Make with AI

Arrange ordinary fabric, see an AI image respond, then change the fabric to guide its next response.

Material action & authenticity8–12 minutes · estimateOptional AI
A

The question

What makes an AI-transformed design feel connected to its heritage source?

We follow the visitor’s actions and reasons. A result may look similar yet lose something that matters. A visibly different result may still carry a connection the visitor values. Neither outcome is required.

AI’s role

A visual partner during making. It transforms a visitor’s tabletop arrangement using one fixed heritage image as a reference. The visitor changes the arrangement after seeing the result. AI does not write an account of how the visitor felt.

THE INTERACTION
Arrange fabric→AI responds→Move a piece→AI responds again

Your movement changes the next input.

Two deliberate snapshots. No typing required.

Illustrative layout. The pattern is a placeholder, not a collection image. Example wording is written for this design, not participant data.

What a visitor does

  1. Choose a source detail

    See one approved textile image and its short background. Point to a detail you want to work with. No written interpretation is required.

  2. Arrange three pieces

    Place, overlap or fold ordinary fabric on a mat in response to the detail. You may point and direct a helper.

  3. Ask AI to respond

    Press Generate when ready. A camera takes one tabletop image. AI receives this layout and the fixed source crop, then returns one new visual response.

  4. Move after looking

    See the source, your layout and the AI result together. Move one piece if you want a different response. You may also keep the result or stop.

  5. Compare the next response

    Generate once more if you changed the layout. Compare the two rounds. Point to a change that matters and say briefly why.

  6. Reflect and take away

    Discuss what still connects to the source and what has changed. Then try the clearly labelled future-display scenario. Keep a visual record with source credit and AI involvement.

The station and the two rounds

You communicate by moving fabric. AI responds with an image while you are making. You can then change the fabric in response to that image. A short conversation happens afterwards so we can understand your choices. Writing a personal explanation is not the task.

  1. Arrange. Put three pieces on a mat. Choose the starting point yourself.
  2. See. Take a snapshot. Compare AI’s result with your layout and the source.
  3. Respond. Move a piece because of what you saw, or decide to keep the result.
  4. Compare. See both rounds. Point to what matters and explain briefly.

At the table: a mat, three or four large fabric pieces, an overhead camera and a screen. The screen has three clearly named areas: Source work, Your layout and AI response. A large Generate button lets the visitor choose when to take each turn. Start with two rounds. Waiting time must be tested.

A real object with its background

The current textile candidate is a wall hanging from Ian Giles’s Everyone Involved, exhibited at God’s House Tower in 2024. Its use of found and vintage fabrics belongs to a work about LGBTQIA+ history and creative life. The venue says the work would enter Southampton City Art Gallery’s collection. One hanging, image permissions and the chosen detail still need agreement. [28]

Show the whole work, the selected crop, the artist and a short checked background together. Do not turn it into an anonymous “British traditional pattern”. A visitor may respond to a fabric relationship or gap without being asked to disclose personal identity. Tudor House needs a confirmed textile reference or permission to host this GHT-associated digital reference with its actual source named.

What each turn gives us

BEFORE GENERATION

Choose a detail

Person: points to an edge, overlap or other detail. They may say what drew their attention.

AI: has not responded yet.

Data: source ID, fact-card version, selected area and any first comment. This gives us the person’s starting reference.

ROUND 1

Layout L1 → image G1

Person: arranges the fabric and presses Generate.

AI: receives the source crop and a photo of L1. It returns one new image, G1, using the layout as a guide.

Data: L1, the actual request, G1, time taken and any help or failure.

ROUND 2

Layout L2 → image G2

Person: moves a piece after seeing G1. They can instead keep G1 or stop.

AI: receives the same source and the new layout. It returns G2 using the same prompt template and settings.

Data: the change from L1 to L2, what prompted it, G2 and the keep, change or stop decision. A second round is never forced.

SHORT REPLAY

Point and explain

Person: points to a change across the source, layouts and outputs.

Researcher: asks “What made you move that?” and “What still connects to the source?”

Data: the pointed area and brief explanation. Record the future-scenario response separately.

Where authenticity becomes a research question

We ask what makes a connection feel real or meaningful to the visitor. The generated image has a known digital source, but the visitor may accept, doubt or ignore its connection to the work. A pleasing picture and a correct source credit are useful observations. Each answers a different question. [1] [2]

Appearance

Does the person point to a recognisable detail, shape or relationship? Example question: “Which part makes you connect these images?” Similarity can matter, but it does not prove shared history.

Making and material

Does an overlap, fold or gap matter because of what they did or noticed in the source? New cloth is ordinary activity material. Touching it does not recreate the source work’s past.

Maker and history

Does the person refer to the artist, the work’s background or a known use of fabric? Keep checked historical information separate from the visitor’s own association. AI must not invent an archival story.

Personal connection

Does the result bring the source closer to their own life, or make it feel less connected? Ask them to explain. Feeling that “I made this” concerns authorship and may or may not involve the heritage source.

These are starting points for analysis. Participants may give other reasons. We do not turn the four areas into a total authenticity score. We first ask open questions and only follow up on a reason the person has introduced.

A concrete example of an authenticity-related episode

Invented example to explain the design, not participant data. A visitor chooses an overlap in the source and leaves a narrow space between two fabric pieces. G1 fills that space. The visitor moves the pieces farther apart, and G2 leaves it open. They explain: “That space is what made me think of the original. Without it, I liked the picture but lost that connection.”

The useful evidence is the chosen source detail, both layouts, both actual outputs and this reason. A comment such as “blue is nicer” would show preference. “The model ignored my move” would show an interaction problem. Neither comment alone supports a claim about authenticity. Do not deliberately create a wrong response to make this episode happen.

What is already in Art Morph, and what we could add

Art Morph already lets museum visitors move everyday materials under a camera and see generated art inspired by the collection. Its official description names real-time generation and a collection-adapted SDXL Turbo model. The developer describes images changing with gestures. We therefore cannot claim that physical materials, a museum setting or AI visual feedback are our invention. [22] [29]

Interaction form

Art Morph documents: material arrangements become collection-inspired generated images.

Our design uses: two deliberate turns, with one named source and both layouts and outputs visible. This makes the sequence easier to discuss and record. Slower snapshots are a practical choice, not a new technique.

The question being investigated

The public Art Morph pages describe: creative interaction with the collection. They do not report an evaluation of how visitors judge authenticity.

Our candidate study asks: which details and relationships visitors use to maintain or change a connection to one source work while making with AI. The public pages cannot tell us whether unpublished Art Morph evaluation covers this question.

The evidence needed

A generated image can show: a visual outcome.

Our analysis also needs: the starting source detail, the movement made after G1, G2 and the visitor’s reason. This can show how a reason changes during interaction. A final satisfaction rating would miss that process.

The possible contribution

A small study could explain when visitors welcome an AI change, correct it or treat it as a separate creation, and what each response rests on. It could guide how museums show sources and visitor contributions in changing displays. These are claims to establish through data and wider comparison.

Published work also covers body-based image generation in public art settings, and CHI 2025’s A(I)Cam used a tangible camera and printed generated images to support reflection. These are close precedents for the interaction form. The Huayao work helps us notice reasons that emerge during making. This targeted comparison does not establish that the proposed authenticity question is untouched. [30] [31] [12]

Smallest build and first checks

AI input: the fixed, permitted source crop and a tabletop photo. Use an image-conditioned generation model. A fixed instruction asks it to use the arrangement as a guide and produce a contemporary visitor response. Do not request a historical reconstruction or add guessed symbols.

Keep steady: the source crop, prompt template, model version, output size and generation strength. Keep the seed fixed if the service supports it. Save all actual settings. Random image differences can still occur, so do not assume every output change was caused by the visitor’s move.

Check action and response first: test moving one large piece while keeping the rest steady. Check whether a visitor can see a useful relationship in the output. If the model repeatedly ignores layouts, try stronger structural guidance before conducting the authenticity study. Log unsuccessful attempts and facilitator help.

First walkthroughs: start with the existing planning estimate of 4–6 people for usability. This is not the final research sample. Ask whether they understand the turns, can decline AI and can explain a specific connection or lack of connection. Only then decide a study scale.

Research record: use a session code. Keep selected snapshots, event times, model and prompt versions, actual outputs, pointed regions and consented short explanations. A still-image sequence is the starting method. Continuous hand video is optional and needs a separate study decision. Keep email addresses out of this record.

Theory and the HCI problem

Connections between people and objects

Relational authenticity asks how a connection becomes meaningful through objects, people, making and history. We follow the connections visitors themselves describe. [1]

Looking alike and having a real link

A similar shape is one possible reason. A known maker, source or history is another. Record these reasons separately. New cloth and generated pixels do not become original heritage material. [2]

Action and response

We examine whether a visitor can see how their material action affects AI, guide the next image and decide when the result no longer fits the source. Embodied generation already has published precedents. [22] [29] [30]

What could be new

The candidate contribution is an account of how visitors maintain or change their grounds for a connection to one named heritage object during material interaction with AI. The interaction technique has close precedents. A contribution would need evidence from the action sequence and the visitor’s reasons. [1] [2] [6] [7] [12] [22] [29] [30]

Input, data and analysis

What we record

Record the source and chosen detail, layout photos L1 and L2, actual outputs G1 and G2, the order and reason for a movement, what the participant points to, short explanations, decisions to keep or stop, and answers to the separate future scenario. Record model settings, waiting time, failed responses and researcher help. A camera log alone cannot explain the visitor’s reason.

How we read the data

Read a complete episode: source detail → first layout → AI response → visitor movement or decision to keep → next response → reason linked to the source. Separate liking an image, controlling AI, feeling authorship and claiming a heritage connection. Retain disagreement, no change, helpful surprises, indifference and non-AI choices.

What would make us revise it

First check whether visitors notice a relationship between their movement and AI’s response. If the model ignores layout, fix the prototype before drawing conclusions about authenticity. If accounts only concern colour preference or novelty, revise the source detail and reflection prompts.

THE SPECULATIVE MOMENT

A future arrangement to discuss

Imagine a future museum where a textile’s digital display keeps changing with each visitor’s fabric arrangement and AI response. A label calls every new result “the same work, still changing”. After your own two rounds, would you keep that label, rename the display as a visitor response, or keep the original work separate?

After the ordinary task, place the two actual outputs beside a clearly fictional future-display label. The changed rule is that a continually remade image is presented as the continuing work. The participant may accept, change or reject that rule. The real source image stays unchanged and credited. The generated result is labelled as a visitor response.

Set-up, participation and the non-AI route

Smallest working set-up

One approved source crop and fact card, three or four easy-to-grasp fabric pieces, a neutral mat, an overhead camera, one screen and one image-to-image generation service. Start with two snapshots. Keep the source crop and generation settings fixed. No live projection, sewing, custom model training or automatic movement recognition is required.

Lowering entry barriers

Offer seated access, large fabric pieces, pointing and a helper who follows the visitor’s directions. No typing, drawing, sewing or personal phone is needed. A visitor can decline photographs and use a researcher-drawn layout. Explain whether that route sends the drawing to AI. A person may decline AI entirely.

If someone declines AI

Make and adjust the physical arrangement, compare it directly with the source and keep a labelled collage record. Record why AI was declined. This route supports participation and is not the same interaction or a randomized control condition.

DIRECTION B · Combines Probes 4 and 5

One Object, Two Interpretations

Exchange and clarify a real interpretation across languages, then turn your approved description into an optional illustrated postcard.

Cross-language exchange6–10 minutes per turn, plus an optional postcard · estimateOptional AI
B

The question

How do visitors retain and negotiate their understanding of a shared heritage object in AI-assisted cross-language exchanges?

The question is whether translated wording remains faithful to a person’s interpretation, how another person understands it, and how different readings can coexist. People do not have to agree on one interpretation.

AI’s role

First, a translation and clarification assistant during a real exchange. Afterwards, an optional image generator makes a postcard illustration from the selected object detail and the participant’s approved description. These are two separate stages with separate records.

ONE SHARED DETAIL DESIGN SKETCH
Same source image
Visitor 1 · original

“It reminds me of a tablecloth at home.”

Optional translation · original kept
Visitor 2 · real reply

“Do you mean its colour or its pattern?”

Reply → clarify → preview a postcard → optional email

Illustrative layout. The pattern is a placeholder, not a collection image. Example wording is written for this design, not participant data.

What a visitor does

  1. See the same object

    Use the same approved image, numbered detail and fact card. Different locations can share the digital reference.

  2. Describe one detail

    Say or write a short observation or association in your own language. Keep the original wording.

  3. Translate and receive a reply

    Choose AI translation, your own language skills or study-team support. Another real visitor identifies the detail and replies. AI does not play the other visitor.

  4. Clarify what you meant

    Confirm, add to or correct the reply. Keep different interpretations where people disagree. Save the completed exchange before generating a postcard.

  5. Create and preview a postcard

    Choose your own approved description and request one illustrated card, or choose a plain card. AI generates a separate response image. The source thumbnail and your exact words are added by a fixed layout.

  6. Choose how to receive it

    Approve the preview. Download it or request a one-off email from the future study service. Email is optional and separate from study participation. No email is sent by this design website.

Talk first, make the postcard afterwards

B connects two people through one object. AI helps with language during their exchange. Once they have replied and clarified, a participant can choose one of their own approved descriptions for a postcard. The image stage is optional. A postcard made before a reply is a personal response card, not a record of a completed exchange.

1 / REAL EXCHANGE

Keep each person’s meaning

Both visitors see the same source and detail number. One describes it. The other replies through optional translation. The sender can clarify. Keep the originals, translations and real reply together. [8] [9]

2 / CHOOSE THE INPUT

Use approved words

Ask the participant to select their own final sentence. The illustration receives the permitted source crop and that sentence. Another person’s words or name are included only if that person agreed. A one-person card is always possible.

3 / GENERATE AND PREVIEW

Check the image separately

Generate one response illustration. Ask “Does this picture fit what you meant?” The person can accept it, remove it or choose the plain card. A retry is optional and recorded. Do not rewrite their approved sentence during image generation.

4 / RECEIVE THE CARD

Download or one-off email

Let the person approve the exact preview, then choose download or a private email request. Email is not shared with the other participant or sent to the image model. The future study system sends only after that visitor requests it.

What the postcard contains

Three parts stay visibly separate: a small unchanged source thumbnail with artist and object credit, the visitor’s exact approved words with an identified translation, and a larger area labelled “AI illustration of my response”. A fixed layout adds the text and credits after generation, so the image model does not redraw names or rewrite the sentence.

The image should connect the permitted source detail with the approved description. For example, if the visitor says a repeated shape reminds them of waves, the illustration can explore that association. The card must identify it as the visitor’s response. It does not claim that the artist intended waves or that the source has a historical sea meaning.

A plain version uses the unchanged source thumbnail and approved words with no generated illustration. Credit text and the AI label remain readable in the saved PNG or PDF. The souvenir does not need to show the complete research transcript. GIFT and museum data souvenirs provide precedents for a personal take-away. They do not establish a new effect from this postcard. [15] [26]

What authenticity means in this stage

The person has already chosen words they accept. The new question is whether an image still expresses that meaning and how it connects to the named object. We keep four judgments separate: whether a source fact is correct, whether a translation fits, whether the other person understands, and whether the author accepts the postcard image. An attractive card may pass one check and fail another. [1] [3]

This keeps B different from A. In A, moving material guides the next AI image and we follow its connection to the source. In B, a real person receives and responds to another person’s meaning. The postcard comes afterwards and helps them keep or share one approved response.

Email receipt in the future study system

  1. Approve the card. Show the exact file that will be delivered. Offer “Download”, “Email me this card” and “Finish without a card”.
  2. Enter an address privately. Only the visitor who requests delivery supplies an address. They confirm the address and request this one message. Do not ask them to enter the other participant’s address.
  3. Send from a private service. A server sends the approved PNG or PDF, or a private expiring link if needed. Email-provider credentials stay on that server. A static GitHub Pages site cannot securely provide this service on its own.
  4. Show the actual status. Distinguish queued, sent and failed. Provider acceptance does not prove that a person received or opened the message. A failure leaves the download option available.
  5. Keep delivery separate. Research records contain the session code and delivery choice. A restricted delivery store holds the address and delivery job. Delete that address after the delivery and agreed support period set in the study plan. Do not use it for other contact or put it in AI prompts, public links or research exports.

Current status: this is a design specification. This public page has no address field, no active model connection and no mail service. A study deployment still needs image permission, model access, private delivery configuration and a tested failure path. No messages have been sent.

Data to collect and questions for a first trial

Exchange record: source and detail ID, language familiarity, original words, translation provider and actual output, real reply, clarification and final accepted meanings. Save pending exchanges as pending.

Postcard record: the exact selected sentence, source-crop version, any permission to use another person’s words, actual generation input and image, preview acceptance, rejection or retry, and the final card version. Keep this after-exchange stage separate in time and analysis.

Delivery record: optional choice and job status under a session code. Email addresses remain outside the analysis dataset. Do not add tracking pixels or infer engagement from message opening.

Useful prompts: “What did the other person understand?” “Did the picture add something you did not mean?” “Which part connects the card to the object?” “Would you prefer the plain card?” Ask about the future display rule after these questions.

First trial: test the exchange before adding generation. Then check whether the postcard is easy to preview, whether declining the image or email feels acceptable, and whether a delivery failure blocks completion. Do not judge the exchange by postcard satisfaction.

Theory and the HCI problem

Relational authenticity

Follow how the object, a person’s experience and the other visitor become connected through exchange. [1] [16]

Personal voice and recognition

Separate the sender’s endorsement of wording, the recipient’s understanding, historical accuracy and the recognition of an interpretation. [3] [17]

Agency and cultural expression

Being able to change a translation can help people express themselves. It can also make a conversation take more effort. AI writing suggestions may remove cultural details. These findings help us choose what to observe. [8] [9] [10]

What could be new

The candidate contribution follows how meaning changes through a real cross-language exchange around one heritage reference. The postcard makes one approved interpretation tangible and may reveal another change when words become an image. Image generation and email delivery are participation features with existing precedents. [1] [3] [8] [9] [10] [16] [17] [25] [26]

Input, data and analysis

What we record

Save the source detail, original message, translation and provider, actual reply and clarification as the exchange record. Separately save the selected postcard text, image input and output, preview acceptance or rejection, and delivery choice. Email addresses belong in a restricted delivery system, not the research transcript or AI prompt.

How we read the data

First analyse how one expression travels through translation, reception and clarification. Then examine whether the optional image represents the participant’s approved meaning. A postcard cannot replace an actual reply, and liking the image cannot prove that the exchange preserved meaning.

What would make us revise it

If there is no real reply, mark the exchange as pending. If postcard generation changes the meaning, let the participant reject the image or choose the plain card. Do not treat image satisfaction, email receipt or a successful translation as one authenticity score.

In the real activity, originals and translations stay visible together. This discussion does not publish messages, alter venue permissions or require consensus.

Interaction revision · 16 Oct 2026

The earlier A asked people to check AI writing, which overlapped with B. New A puts AI inside a two-round material interaction. B adds an optional illustrated postcard and a separate email choice after a real exchange. The five-probe table now explains each case and design choice in everyday words.

The additional selection criterion

Make participation possible for more people. Reduce demands on heritage knowledge, language fluency, manual skill, personal devices, friendship pairs and willingness to use AI. This is a design aim to test, not a claim that either activity already works for everyone.

The resulting decision: keep 1 as an introduction, develop 2 as A, pause 3 as a standalone activity, and combine 4 with 5 as B. A take-away supports participation. The research follows actions and reasons in A, and real interpretation and replies in B.

How each piece of feedback changes the design
“Could visitors take something away?”
A can keep a visual record of the two rounds. B adds an optional postcard after the exchange. The record supports a conversation about what happened.
“How can two places see one object?”
B uses the same image, numbered detail and checked fact card in both places. We are studying digital exchange, not moving an original object.
“Can someone choose not to use AI?”
Both routes remain complete without AI. In B, human language support is available when necessary and recorded.
“What role does AI actually play?”
A turns material layouts into visual responses while the visitor is making. B translates during the exchange, then optionally illustrates an approved description. Neither AI role decides whether a heritage object is authentic.
“What about clothing or AI-generated expressions?”
Keep these as later variants. A can optionally compare an arrangement with a sleeve edge. B could add a participant-chosen expression. Full virtual try-on and generated stickers are not necessary for the first study. Emoji interpretation can differ. [18] [22]

02 · Make with AI

Early version: make a fabric shape, then check AI’s written account. New A: arrange three fabric pieces, generate an image, move a piece after seeing it, then compare the next image.

We collect: the source detail, both layouts, both actual AI outputs, movements, choices and brief reasons.

01 / MAKING

Huayao embroidery: what making reveals, and why it matters for A

The original study and what it could observe

The original question concerned forms of creativity when artisans use embodied craft skills with generative AI fine-tuned on their own heritage. Eight Huayao embroiderers worked with the research team. A six-month, four-stage process included shared learning, preparing 152 embroidery images, configuring three generation workflows, co-creating images and embroidering a chosen image. The final in-situ stages took place over eight days. Researchers interviewed people during making, held a focus group and analysed recordings, transcripts and notes using open coding. [12]

Original study: generated pattern → embroidery → adjustment → explanation of the adjustment

What the original research supports

  1. AI supplied a starting point that people could judge and revise.

    Participants described faster visualisation and new ideas, while continuing to select, discard and modify generated images. This supports treating generation as part of an unfolding practice. It does not establish a measured productivity gain or show that the selected image was already culturally satisfactory.

  2. Judgment drew on local knowledge and practical making, not only visual similarity.

    Participants discussed whether animals looked like local animals and seemed to be doing something, whether recognisable motifs were present, and whether density, outlines and composition worked for embroidery. Dianhua explained that she still needed to change a generated image when embroidering it. Their reasons came from local knowledge and making. The study does not give one authenticity rule for everyone.

  3. Making exposed judgments that image evaluation alone might miss.

    In the discussion, the authors describe how attention to outlines and shading became clearer through making. They ask whether an image represented Huayao culture in advance, or whether embroiderers brought it into relation with that culture through reconfiguration and reinterpretation. This is an interpretive question arising from the process, not a demonstrated answer that every handmade modification makes an image authentic.

A participant’s account. Dianhua: “I still have to change it a bit”. The authors’ discussion. “many of the modifications made during the making offer insights”. These are short excerpts. The first concerns modifying an image. The second is the authors’ interpretation of what the process made visible. [12]

Figure 5 makes the discarded image, selected image and finished embroidery available for comparison. A visible difference shows that something changed. Its cultural or practical meaning comes from the participant accounts and the researchers’ analysis, not from the photograph alone.

What this does not establish. This is a preliminary CHI extended abstract with eight skilled practitioners and no image-only control condition that would establish the causal contribution of making. Participants’ reports, the researchers’ observations and the authors’ theoretical interpretation should not be treated as the same kind of evidence. The study did not test ordinary museum visitors, AI-written experience cards or an increase in visitor authenticity.

What this suggests for the new A

The useful idea is to follow a change through making and ask why it matters. In the Huayao study, experienced embroiderers selected generated images and changed them while stitching. New A lets a museum visitor move simple materials between two AI image responses. The skills, duration and setting differ. This is a reason to try the design, not evidence that it will work.

New A: source detail → layout L1 → generated image G1 → visitor movement → layout L2 → generated image G2 → brief explanation

Save the actual images and the visitor’s reason. A reopened space might matter because it recalls the chosen object detail. It might simply look nicer. Only the participant’s account can help distinguish these reasons. A short material action does not transfer an embroiderer’s expertise or give new cloth a historical origin.

Our earlier A used AI to write a making record. That overlapped with B’s focus on personal meaning. The current A uses AI during the making itself. Its unresolved question concerns how a visitor maintains or changes a connection to a source work through actions and responses. The authenticity guide and comparison with Art Morph explain what data would be needed. [1] [2] [12] [22] [29] [30]

03 / CROSS-LANGUAGE AGENCY

Translation control has benefits and costs

What the research supports

In 45 dyads of Chinese new immigrants and English-speaking local residents in the US, post-editing conditions enabled greater exercise of agency, with costs to dyadic communication. The figure shows conditions. The findings come from the paper’s results and discussion. [9]

What it leaves for B

Let people clarify in their own language. Observe the recipient’s actual reply and the sender’s repair. We still need to learn whether this is manageable during a short museum encounter and how it affects the sense that a personal interpretation has been retained.

This was everyday information exchange, not a museum or a China–UK comparison. Other research warns that AI suggestions can diminish cultural nuances and that colour-name translation need not preserve perceptual correspondence. These are questions to investigate, not predicted museum effects. [8] [10]

What real museums already do

Cleveland Museum of Art

Talk to the Art, Art Morph and Extend the Art provide precedents for AI questions, generation and extension. These forms are already in use, so the question must go beyond adding AI. Endless Threads uses a camera and OpenCV, with no generative-AI virtual try-on claim. [22] [23] [24]

GIFT and museum souvenirs

GIFT links objects and personal messages. Printed data souvenirs already connect a museum visit with a personal record. These projects support the card and interpersonal forms, but were not the proposed generative-AI exchanges. [15] [16] [26]

Nasher Museum

AI-generated curatorial text is a real precedent. It does not demonstrate that two visitors retain their meanings through translation, reply and clarification. [25]

Other findings that constrain our claims

AI co-writing research distinguishes authors’ experiences from readers’ judgments. Heritage narrative research suggests that unfamiliarity can make generated accounts easier to accept. Public attitudes to conversational heritage agents are not deployment effects. [3] [4] [13]

TwistLens studies a specific pre-visit preview task and Gen-Diaolou concerns learning and preservation awareness. Neither licenses a general claim that image editing improves authenticity. HarmonyCut informs structured guidance, without requiring us to copy a complex authoring system. [5] [14] [19]

Research on perceived human versus AI empathy suggests that source attribution can matter in another context. It does not establish an effect in heritage visits. B records wording, translation and image generation separately. A records layouts, actual outputs and reasons separately. [20]

05 / SPECULATION

The original words and our design reasoning

Speculation means making an imagined situation clear enough to try and discuss. Here we ask how a future museum could work differently, then use the activity to help people judge that possibility.

For A

A possible future museum calls a continually remade digital display the same work. The two actual outputs and a fictional label help visitors discuss what still connects to the source.

For B

A possible future museum shows AI translation as the default while originals are filed separately. The participant considers who may authorize that visibility rule. The actual exchange keeps originals and translations together.

Sequence matters. Complete the ordinary activity first. Clearly introduce the future scenario afterwards. Separate current usability feedback from judgments about the proposed arrangement. If people only discuss convenience and never engage with another arrangement, we cannot claim that the speculative element worked.

The thirteen passages below combine seven method passages, a selection principle and five case-linked passages. All are original sentences from Anthony Dunne and Fiona Raby’s Speculative Everything (2013). Pages are printed book pages, checked against the supplied pages. Footnote markers are omitted. The meanings and applications are our interpretations. The book does not itself propose either AI museum activity. The book cases help us make design choices. They do not prove that our designs are new or that visitors will respond as expected. The case images below are credited screenshots of the book figures. [21]

How selection uses speculation. Identify a present default, a changed rule, a material or digital prop, a concrete trade-off and room for a visitor to refuse. All five table rows now make this reasoning explicit. A uses actual image changes to discuss a changing museum work. B uses the exchange and postcard to discuss whose meaning a museum shows. We also consider the research question, participant input and ease of joining.

Passage 1 · pp. 2–3Use possible futures to understand the present

What we are interested in, though, is the idea of possible futures and using them as tools to better understand the present and to discuss the kind of future people want, and, of course, ones people do not want.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 1, pp. 2–3. [21]

What this supports

A possible future helps people examine present choices. It is not a prediction.

Our use and its limit

A asks what happens when a museum work becomes a changing visitor-and-AI display. B asks whose words remain visible across languages. These are our proposed museum situations.

Passage 2 · pp. 3A future is a medium for thinking

For us futures are not a destination or something to be strived for but a medium to aid imaginative thought—to speculate with.

A possible future lets people think about a way of living and decide what they want to keep or change.

It does not show that emojis have the same meaning for everyone. The postcard is not proof that two people understood each other.

06 / OBJECTS AND THE NEXT STEP

Use digital material from the target venues

Ordinary cloth is an activity material. It is not a heritage object, and the study does not require handling original collections.

Preferred textile candidate

Everyone Involved, Ian Giles, shown at God’s House Tower in 2024, includes wall hangings made with vintage and found fabrics. The venue states that the work would enter Southampton City Art Gallery’s collection after the exhibition. This does not establish GHT ownership or current display. [28]

Select one hanging, a meaningful detail and its contextual information with the venue and artist. Digital image access, use permission and the checked fact card remain to be confirmed. Keep the work’s creative and community context visible.

Tudor House & Garden

The Leather Jug is an official collection highlight, but it is leather, not a textile. Its clothing-like form can inform a non-textile variant. A strictly textile-led study needs a confirmed textile object or permission to use the GHT-associated digital material at this site with its actual source named. [27]

No verified British–Chinese textile pair has been established at these two venues. B can use one shared object across languages without pretending that nationality determines cultural meaning. The implementation site, exhibition venue and collecting institution must be recorded separately.

A small first trial

  1. Confirm one digital object, the usable image, its context and its fact card.
  2. Run 4–6 usability walkthroughs of each direction. This is a planning estimate, not a final research sample.
  3. Check whether people understand the task, contribute specific reasons and can decline AI without being blocked.
  4. For B, obtain several completed real reply chains. A pending message is not a completed exchange.
  5. Revise the activities before fixing the final study scale or a comparison design.

These two self-selected routes help investigate participation and interpretation. They do not establish the causal effect of AI. Detailed theory, data and revision criteria are inside each direction above.
