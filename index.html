SECOND-ROUND PROBES · CURRENT DESIGN

What feels authentic
when AI joins the visit?

Two short activities explore how people connect with heritage objects, express their own experience and understand another visitor.

A probe gives someone something to try and talk about. Here, “authenticity” is an open question about a real or meaningful connection with an object, its history or other people. We ask visitors what that connection rests on.

The wider research question and what “authenticity” means here

How do human–AI interactions shape visitors’ experiences of authenticity in museums and heritage sites?

The two supporting questions ask what visitors draw on for authenticity, and how they negotiate different senses of authenticity when interpreting heritage objects with AI. A and B are focused parts of this larger project.

We do not treat historical accuracy, resemblance, felt connection, personal voice and another person’s understanding as interchangeable. Their relationships are what the activities help us examine. The theories guide attention rather than prescribe what visitors must feel. [1] [2] [3] [17]

01 / THE TWO CURRENT DIRECTIONS

Two ways into the same research problem

Choose A to explore lived making and its representation. Choose B to explore personal meaning through cross-language exchange. Visitors take part in one short activity, not a five-part programme.

DIRECTION A · Developed from Probe 2

My Making Card

Try a simple material action, then decide whether AI describes your experience in words you recognize.

Making & authenticity8–12 minutes · estimateOptional AI
A

The question

How do visitors form or revise a sense of authenticity through hands-on making and AI interpretation?

A visitor may think AI described the shape correctly but missed what making it felt like. This activity follows the reasons behind that distinction and its connection to the heritage object.

AI’s role

A correctable visual-comparison and recording assistant. It receives the approved object image, checked fact card, making image and the visitor’s own description. It returns one visual observation and one experience note. Touch and emotion must come from the visitor’s input, not be inferred from a photograph.

MY MAKING CARD DESIGN SKETCH
Object detail
My material response

“The fold took more effort than I expected.”

My approved words · object source · AI involvement

Illustrative layout. The pattern is a placeholder, not a collection image. Example wording is written for this design, not participant data.

What a visitor does

  1. Notice a detail

    Look at an approved object image, one detail and a short checked fact card. Say or write what you notice before AI offers an explanation.

  2. Try one material action

    Fold, arrange or layer ordinary cloth or paper in response to the detail. Say one thing the action made you feel or think. It is a response to the object, not a historical replica.

  3. Choose assistance

    Ask AI to help record the experience, or write it yourself. A researcher can help photograph the tabletop arrangement or enter your words. Neither face nor body photography is required.

  4. Review the account

    See your original words beside AI’s visual observation and experience note. Keep, change or delete a sentence. You can simply say “That is not what I meant”.

  5. Make the card

    Keep one object detail, a making photo or diagram, your approved sentence and the object source. Mark AI involvement. Both routes receive an A6 or digital card.

  6. Reflect, then speculate

    First ask which part best represents the experience and how it relates to the object. Then introduce the future scenario below. Mark ordinary activity and speculative discussion separately.

Theory and the HCI problem

Relational authenticity

Trace connections among the person, object, making, history and personal experience. Do not reduce authenticity to a correct fact or an attractive image. [1]

Resemblance and provenance

Separate a similar-looking form from an evidenced connection to the source object. Ordinary cloth does not acquire the object’s historical material status. [2]

Authentic personal expression

Ask whether the participant recognizes and endorses the AI-assisted account. The AI’s factual accuracy and the person’s sense of being represented are related but distinct judgments. [3]

What could be new

The candidate knowledge gain is how visitors authorize, contest or revise an AI account of their own short making experience, and what that reveals about their connection with the object. It is not the first use of AI with cloth or of a printed museum souvenir. [1] [2] [3] [6] [7] [12] [15] [22]

Input, data and analysis

What we record

Object and fact-card versions, the first observation, the material action and any assistance, participant wording, actual AI input and output, accepted or rejected text, edits and reasons, the final account, and the choice to use or decline AI. Record photo, research-retention and take-away preferences separately.

How we read the data

Use a reasoned acceptance, correction or rejection as an episode. Follow the grounds the visitor invokes, the AI account, the response and the resulting record. Distinguish visual errors, a poor account of experience, a mistaken source claim and aesthetic preference. Include cases with no change.

What would make us revise it

If the activity only produces “fun” or “AI has no sense of touch”, revise the prompts. We need specific accounts of making, the source object and why an AI description does or does not fit.

THE SPECULATIVE MOMENT

A future arrangement to discuss

Imagine that future museums give every visitor an AI-written making card, and this becomes the main record through which other people understand that visit. Which parts may AI describe for you, and which should remain yours to say?

The change being discussed is who may record and represent lived experience. The card is a prop for that arrangement. Cloth, AI and printing are not in themselves the speculative element. Visitors can prefer a self-written account or reject the proposed arrangement entirely.

Original support: Passage 1, Passage 3, Passage 4, Passage 5, Passage 6 from Dunne & Raby. [21]

Set-up, participation and the non-AI route

Smallest working set-up

One approved digital object and fact card, pre-cut cloth or paper, a tablet or laptop, a tabletop photo option, a constrained multimodal AI prompt and a card template. Printing is optional. Card layout needs no second generative model.

Lowering entry barriers

No sewing, drawing, prompting or personal device is required. Visitors can arrange shapes, point or direct another person. Permit speech, writing and researcher transcription. A failed camera or printer should not stop participation.

If someone declines AI

The visitor records their own sentence and still completes the same card. A non-AI route reveals choices and reasons. Because people choose their route, it is not a randomized test of AI’s effects.

DIRECTION B · Combines Probes 4 and 5

One Object, Two Interpretations

Explain a detail in your own language, read another visitor’s reply through optional AI translation, then clarify what you meant.

Cross-language exchange6–10 minutes per turn · estimateOptional AI
B

The question

How do visitors retain and negotiate their understanding of a shared heritage object in AI-assisted cross-language exchanges?

The question is whether translated wording remains faithful to a person’s interpretation, how another person understands it, and how different readings can coexist. People do not have to agree on one interpretation.

AI’s role

A translation and wording-clarification assistant. It aims to preserve personal meaning and uncertainty. It does not invent cultural symbolism, speak for a national culture or merge different views into one authoritative interpretation. One concise translation is the default.

“Do you mean its colour or its pattern?”

Reply → clarify → keep both interpretations

Illustrative layout. The pattern is a placeholder, not a collection image. Example wording is written for this design, not participant data.

What a visitor does

  1. Share the same reference

    Use the same approved image, detail numbers and fact-card version at both locations. A single physical object need not travel. This studies exchange around digital material.

  2. Explain in your own language

    Choose a detail and say “I notice…” or “It reminds me of…”. Keep the original wording. A short sentence or pointing with researcher transcription is enough.

  3. Choose language support

    Use AI translation, express the message yourself or request human language support. Keep original and translated wording together and name the provider. Showing unreadable text is not a completed non-AI exchange.

  4. Receive a real reply

    Another participant identifies the detail and says “I understand you to mean…” or asks a question. Messages may come from consenting earlier visitors. AI must not impersonate a remote participant.

  5. Confirm or clarify

    The sender accepts, adds, corrects or keeps a different interpretation. A brief clarification in their own language can update the translation. Do not require editing an unfamiliar foreign language.

  6. Keep the conversation

    Keep both original messages, identified translations and the reply on a conversation card. Reflect on meaning changes, then discuss the future scenario. An unmatched message remains pending, not a completed two-way exchange.

Theory and the HCI problem

Relational authenticity

Follow how the object, a person’s experience and the other visitor become connected through exchange. [1] [16]

Personal voice and recognition

Separate the sender’s endorsement of wording, the recipient’s understanding, historical accuracy and the recognition of an interpretation. [3] [17]

Agency and cultural expression

Translation control may carry communication costs. Fluent suggestions can weaken cultural detail. These findings guide what to observe, rather than predict the museum result. [8] [9] [10]

What could be new

The candidate contribution follows a complete expression–translation–reception–clarification sequence around one heritage reference. Moving an existing translator to two countries or adding an object is not sufficient novelty on its own. [1] [3] [8] [9] [10] [16] [17] [25] [26]

Input, data and analysis

What we record

Shared object and detail ID, fact-card version, original wording, translation provider, actual model and prompt version, AI choice and reason, sent text, the recipient’s stated understanding, reply, clarification and final parallel texts. Record language familiarity and relevant experience without using nationality as a substitute for cultural viewpoint.

How we read the data

Follow a specific expression through wording, translation, reception and repair. Examine references, colour words, tone, memories and symbolic claims. Historical truth, translation fit, author endorsement and recipient understanding are separate judgments, not one authenticity score. An accurately understood disagreement is not automatically failure.

What would make us revise it

If the activity only records whether translation is correct, make the shared detail and the real reply more explicit. Without an actual reply, the data can address expression and translation but not two-way negotiation.

THE SPECULATIVE MOMENT

A future arrangement to discuss

Imagine a future museum where the same object keeps visitor interpretations from different places and languages. AI makes them readable to one another. Who should decide which versions stay, which are changed, and whether any should be merged?

The alternative arrangement is a museum retaining parallel visitor interpretations. The conversation card makes this arrangement visible. Participants may prefer separate institutional facts and visitor comments, decline AI co-writing, or reject the future arrangement. Consensus is not the required ending.

Original support: Passage 1, Passage 2, Passage 5, Passage 6, Passage 7 from Dunne & Raby. [21]

Set-up, participation and the non-AI route

Smallest working set-up

One approved digital object, a checked fact card, detail numbers, short messages, optional translation, a small consented message pool and a card template. Start with different-language speakers at one site, then coordinate exchange across locations. A full social network is unnecessary.

Lowering entry barriers

People can come alone and reply asynchronously. They do not need to know the recipient or be online together. Permit short text, speech and transcription. Keep actual language differences separate from differences between sites.

If someone declines AI

Participants can write directly or use human translation provided by the study team. Record the provider and time required. Back-translation may help a person check, but it does not prove accuracy. The recipient’s actual response supplies the evidence of understanding.

02 / THE ITERATION

Why five became two

This is a traceable design decision, not a ranking of tested outcomes.

01

Five starting points

Ask, make, change, co-write and share. The earlier atlas explains all five initial designs. It remains available as the starting point, rather than the current recommendation.

02

Concept feedback · 9 Oct 2026

One researcher questioned the need for 1 and 3, suggested a card to take away, saw overlap between 4 and 5, and asked how people in two places could see the same object. This is concept feedback, not a visitor trial.

03

Supervisor guidance

Broaden participation, make AI’s role explicit, explore links with clothing and multilingual exchange, and consider merging 4 and 5. The date of this guidance was not recorded, so it is kept separate from the 9 October feedback.

04

Two directions · 14–16 Oct 2026

Develop 2 as A, focused on making and authenticity. Merge 4 and 5 as B, focused on cross-language interpretation. Add a clearer activity, a real AI role, participant input, a take-away record and an explicit speculative discussion.

The additional selection criterion

Make participation possible for more people. Reduce demands on heritage knowledge, language fluency, manual skill, personal devices, friendship pairs and willingness to use AI. This is a design aim to test, not a claim that either activity already works for everyone.

The resulting decision: keep 1 as an introduction, develop 2 as A, pause 3 as a standalone activity, and combine 4 with 5 as B. A take-away card supports participation but is not the research contribution.

How each piece of feedback changes the design
“Could visitors take something away?”
Both directions end with a card. The research follows how its wording is chosen and endorsed, rather than the novelty of printing.
“How can two places see one object?”
B uses the same image, numbered detail and checked fact card in both places. We are studying digital exchange, not moving an original object.
“Can someone choose not to use AI?”
Both routes remain complete without AI. In B, human language support is available when necessary and recorded.
“What role does AI actually play?”
A uses visual comparison and recording. B uses translation and clarification. Neither role is an authority on historical authenticity or national cultural meaning.
“What about clothing or AI-generated expressions?”
Keep these as later variants. A can optionally compare an arrangement with a sleeve edge. B could add a participant-chosen expression. Full virtual try-on and generated stickers are not necessary for the first study. Emoji interpretation can differ. [18] [22]

The 9 October record is a recollected concept-feedback summary, not a transcript. Supervisor guidance is kept as a separate source of design input. No interview recordings or individual participant records are published here.

04 / RESEARCH AND REAL PRACTICE

What the evidence supports

Original paper figures show the interaction forms. The interpretation below is ours, with the transfer limits kept visible.

01 / MAKING

Huayao embroidery: generation is not the end of making

What the research supports

Embroiderers selected and modified generated patterns through making, drawing on cultural knowledge, liveliness and practical stitchability. The image and the finished embroidery are not identical. This is preliminary work with eight embroiderers, published as a CHI extended abstract. [12]

What it leaves for A

Can ordinary museum visitors explain why an AI account fits or misses their own short material experience? Retain the action, the first-person account and the correction. Artisan cultural judgment is not a substitute for visitor experience.

Related culturally situated Huayao work adds process evidence. The associated papers share a team and research programme and are not independent replications. ZOMA also offers a hybrid craft precedent, but our reading of ZOMA is limited to its published abstract. [6] [7]

02 / SOCIAL RECOGNITION

The AI Pattern Merchant: a working intervention inside craft relationships

What the research supports

The speculative intervention put generation and printing into real relationships of craft, exchange and recognition. A functional prototype can be part of speculation when it opens a different arrangement to discussion. [11]

What it leaves for A and B

A needs a question about who may represent lived experience. B needs a question about whose interpretations are retained. Ordinary printing or comments alone do not establish speculation. The authors reported attention to the inserted samples but no purchases, so the stall image is not proof of community acceptance.

The formal DRS DOI ends in 1403. The reproduced figures come from the author version, whose cover retained an older DOI. We cite the formal metadata.

03 / CROSS-LANGUAGE AGENCY

Translation control has benefits and costs

What the research supports

In 45 dyads of Chinese new immigrants and English-speaking local residents in the US, post-editing conditions enabled greater exercise of agency, with costs to dyadic communication. The figure shows conditions. The findings come from the paper’s results and discussion. [9]

What it leaves for B

Let people clarify in their own language. Observe the recipient’s actual reply and the sender’s repair. We still need to learn whether this is manageable during a short museum encounter and how it affects the sense that a personal interpretation has been retained.

This was everyday information exchange, not a museum or a China–UK comparison. Other research warns that AI suggestions can diminish cultural nuances and that colour-name translation need not preserve perceptual correspondence. These are questions to investigate, not predicted museum effects. [8] [10]

What real museums already do

Cleveland Museum of Art

Talk to the Art, Art Morph and Extend the Art provide precedents for AI questions, generation and extension. These forms are already in use, so the question must go beyond adding AI. Endless Threads uses a camera and OpenCV, rather than evidence of generative-AI virtual try-on. [22] [23] [24]

GIFT and museum souvenirs

GIFT links objects and personal messages. Printed data souvenirs already connect a museum visit with a personal record. These projects support the card and interpersonal forms, but were not the proposed generative-AI exchanges. [15] [16] [26]

Nasher Museum

AI-generated curatorial text is a real precedent. It does not demonstrate that two visitors retain their meanings through translation, reply and clarification. [25]

Other findings that constrain our claims

AI co-writing research distinguishes authors’ experiences from readers’ judgments. Heritage narrative research suggests that unfamiliarity can make generated accounts easier to accept. Public attitudes to conversational heritage agents are not deployment effects. [3] [4] [13]

TwistLens studies a specific pre-visit preview task and Gen-Diaolou concerns learning and preservation awareness. Neither licenses a general claim that image editing improves authenticity. HarmonyCut informs structured guidance, without requiring us to copy a complex authoring system. [5] [14] [19]

Research on perceived human versus AI empathy suggests that source attribution can matter in another context. It does not establish an effect in heritage visits. A and B record wording and AI involvement separately. [20]

05 / SPECULATION

The original words and our design reasoning

Speculation here means using a possible arrangement to question the present. It is a method within the probes, not a synonym for adding AI.

For A

The future museum may use an AI-written card as the main record of a visitor’s making. The question is who may describe and represent that experience.

For B

The future museum may retain parallel visitor interpretations across languages. The question is who decides what stays and whether differences are merged.

Sequence matters. Complete the ordinary activity first. Clearly introduce the future scenario afterwards. Separate current usability feedback from judgments about the proposed arrangement. If people only discuss convenience and never engage with another arrangement, we cannot claim that the speculative element worked.

The seven passages below are original sentences from Anthony Dunne and Fiona Raby’s Speculative Everything (2013). Pages are printed book pages, checked against the supplied pages. Footnote markers are omitted. The meanings and applications are our interpretations. The book does not itself propose either AI museum activity. [21]

Passage 1 · pp. 2–3Use possible futures to understand the present
What we are interested in, though, is the idea of possible futures and using them as tools to better understand the present and to discuss the kind of future people want, and, of course, ones people do not want.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 1, pp. 2–3. [21]

What this supports

A possible future helps people examine present choices. It is not a prediction.

Our use and its limit

A asks who may record a visitor’s experience. B asks whose interpretations a museum should retain. These proposed arrangements are ours, rather than examples from the book.

Passage 2 · pp. 3A future is a medium for thinking
For us futures are not a destination or something to be strived for but a medium to aid imaginative thought—to speculate with.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 1, pp. 3. [21]

What this supports

The purpose is to think through an arrangement, rather than demonstrate the preferred future.

Our use and its limit

After the ordinary activity, explicitly introduce a future museum arrangement. Ask what it would change, what visitors would keep, and what they would refuse. A routine AI task alone is not enough.

Passage 3 · pp. 90A prop makes another possibility discussable
They help us think about alternative possibilities—they challenge the ideals, values, and beliefs of our society embodied in material culture.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 6, pp. 90. [21]

What this supports

The object supports reflection on the values embodied in a possible arrangement.

Our use and its limit

A’s experience card makes a different way of recording a visit visible. B’s parallel conversation card makes a different way of retaining interpretations visible. A souvenir or image edit alone does not establish this function.

Passage 4 · pp. 92A prop can actually work
For some, the term prop means a fake object, something that doesn’t work, but in the context of a speculative design, a prop can be a fully working prototype or not.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 6, pp. 92. [21]

What this supports

Working technology is compatible with a speculative prop. Functionality is not the deciding criterion.

Our use and its limit

Run the actual AI in A and B, retain its real outputs and visitor changes, and use the resulting card to discuss the alternative arrangement. This does not make every working AI prototype speculative.

Passage 5 · pp. 93Leave space for people’s own interpretations
Prefigurative futures (or pre-enactments?) also use props to transport viewers’ imagination but, whereas in this role they are bringing a specific future to the present so that it can be partially experienced in advance, user-tested, or prepared for, we are more interested in using props to transport viewers’ imagination into a thought experiment, or what-if, and allow enough room for them to make their own interpretations.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 6, pp. 93. [21]

What this supports

Participants should interpret a thought experiment rather than rehearse a future already decided by the researcher.

Our use and its limit

A permits approval, correction, rejection or indifference. B permits disagreement after mutual understanding. Do not require visitors to conclude that AI damages authenticity. Applying the prop idea to digital cards is our methodological transfer.

Passage 6 · pp. 161People may make their own judgments
We view people as free agents, not necessarily rational, but free to make up their own minds.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 9, pp. 161. [21]

What this supports

This is a commitment to participants’ freedom to judge and choose.

Our use and its limit

Both directions allow a non-AI route. Visitors can reject wording or reject the future arrangement. Receiving a card does not depend on using AI. This principle does not establish universal accessibility or a causal comparison between self-selected routes.

Passage 7 · pp. 2Create a space for discussion
This form of design thrives on imagination and aims to open up new perspectives on what are sometimes called wicked problems, to create spaces for discussion and debate about alternative ways of being, and to inspire and encourage people’s imaginations to flow freely.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 1, pp. 2. [21]

What this supports

Speculation opens discussion of alternatives rather than merely evaluating a product feature.

Our use and its limit

B asks whether different interpretations should remain side by side and who decides what is kept. A asks whether an AI account can represent a lived experience. The book does not itself propose AI museum cards or multilingual labels.

06 / OBJECTS AND THE NEXT STEP

Use digital material from the target venues

Ordinary cloth is an activity material. It is not a heritage object, and the study does not require handling original collections.

Preferred textile candidate

Everyone Involved, Ian Giles, shown at God’s House Tower in 2024, includes wall hangings made with vintage and found fabrics. The venue states that the work would enter Southampton City Art Gallery’s collection after the exhibition. This does not establish GHT ownership or current display. [28]

Select one hanging, a meaningful detail and its contextual information with the venue and artist. Digital image access, use permission and the checked fact card remain to be confirmed. Keep the work’s creative and community context visible.

Tudor House & Garden

The Leather Jug is an official collection highlight, but it is leather, not a textile. Its clothing-like form can inform a non-textile variant. A strictly textile-led study needs a confirmed textile object or permission to use the GHT-associated digital material at this site with its actual source named. [27]

No verified British–Chinese textile pair has been established at these two venues. B can use one shared object across languages without pretending that nationality determines cultural meaning. The implementation site, exhibition venue and collecting institution must be recorded separately.

A small first trial

  1. Confirm one digital object, the usable image, its context and its fact card.
  2. Run 4–6 usability walkthroughs of each direction. This is a planning estimate, not a final research sample.
  3. Check whether people understand the task, contribute specific reasons and can decline AI without being blocked.
  4. For B, obtain several completed real reply chains. A pending message is not a completed exchange.
  5. Revise the activities before fixing the final study scale or a comparison design.

These two self-selected routes help investigate participation and interpretation. They do not establish the causal effect of AI. Detailed theory, data and revision criteria are inside each direction above.
