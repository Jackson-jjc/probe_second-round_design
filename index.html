What feels authentic
when AI joins the visit?

Two short activities explore how people connect with heritage objects, express their own experience and understand another visitor.

A probe gives someone something to try and talk about. Here, “authenticity” is an open question about a real or meaningful connection with an object, its history or other people. We ask visitors what that connection rests on.

The wider research question and what “authenticity” means here

How do human–AI interactions shape visitors’ experiences of authenticity in museums and heritage sites?

The two supporting questions ask what visitors draw on for authenticity, and how they negotiate different senses of authenticity when interpreting heritage objects with AI. A and B are focused parts of this larger project.

We examine historical accuracy, resemblance, felt connection, personal voice and another person’s understanding as distinct judgments. The activities show how they relate. The theories help us decide what to notice. Visitors can give their own reasons and feelings. [1] [2] [3] [17]

01 / THE TWO CURRENT DIRECTIONS

Two ways into the same research problem

Both use one small cuff or bag area at the same capture station. In A, a physical action guides AI’s next preview. In B, a real conversation shapes one developing postcard. Visitors choose one short activity.

DIRECTION A · Developed from Probe 2

Make with AI on My Cuff or Bag

Choose a cuff or bag corner, see an AI pattern preview, then fold or reposition the fabric to guide its next response.

Material action & authenticity8–12 minutes · estimateOptional AI
A

The question

What makes an AI-adapted pattern on a personal item feel connected to its heritage source?

We follow how a pattern moves from a named source work into someone’s everyday life. A visitor can value a familiar shape, a material relationship, the artist’s story or a personal memory. Their actions and reasons show which connection matters.

AI’s role

A visual partner during the local material action. It proposes a pattern adaptation for the captured cuff or bag area, using a fixed source detail. The visitor’s next fold or position becomes its next input. Added patterns appear in digital previews.

THE INTERACTION
Frame a cuff or bag→AI responds→Fold or reposition→AI responds again

One local area. Your action guides the next input.

Two deliberate snapshots. No typing required.

Design sketch with invented patterns and example wording.

What a visitor does

  1. Choose a source and item

    Look at the named textile work and its background. Point to one detail, then choose a sleeve cuff or bag corner. A plain study item is available.

  2. Frame one local area

    Place the item inside the fixed tabletop window. Check the crop preview, then press Capture. Only this selected crop C1 enters the image request and study record.

  3. See the first response

    AI uses the source detail and C1 to propose a digital pattern adaptation G1 on the local item. The screen keeps the source, captured crop and generated preview visible together.

  4. Respond with an action

    Fold the cuff, flatten a bag edge or reposition the fabric after seeing G1. Capture C2 in the same window when ready. You can also keep G1.

  5. Compare and choose

    AI responds to C2 as G2. Compare the two previews and point to a change that matters. Explain briefly what connects it to the source or your own life.

  6. Discuss and keep a record

    Use the previews and a fictional future label to discuss changing museum works. Keep a visual record with source credit and your selected version.

One small place to interact

Place a sleeve cuff or bag corner inside a fixed capture window. Both A and B use this station. Check the local preview and press Capture. The system crops to the selected window before AI input or storage. A plain study item, prepared photograph or outline offers another way to join.

  1. Frame. Approve local crop C1.
  2. See. AI uses source S and C1 to propose G1.
  3. Act. Fold or reposition, then capture C2.
  4. Compare. Point to a meaningful change in G2.

Starting set-up: a fixed camera, an approximately 20 × 20 cm tabletop window, one screen and a Capture button. The screen keeps the source, approved local crop and generated preview side by side. Pilot sessions establish the useful window size and waiting time.

A named work enters everyday life

The current textile candidate is a wall hanging from Ian Giles’s Everyone Involved, exhibited at God’s House Tower in 2024. Its found and vintage fabrics belong to a work about LGBTQIA+ history and creative life. The venue states that it would enter Southampton City Art Gallery’s collection. Agree one hanging, its usable image and a detail with the relevant people. Show the artist and short checked background beside the crop. [28]

The visitor considers what this detail could mean on their own cuff or bag. The work’s documented background and the visitor’s associations remain clearly identified. Tudor House can host the same permitted digital reference with its actual source named, or use a confirmed textile reference of its own.

SHORT REPLAY

Point and explain

Person: points across the source and previews.

Researcher: asks “What made you change that?” and “What still connects to the source?”

Data: pointed area, brief reason and separately labelled future-scenario answers.

How authenticity enters the activity

Ask what makes the source connection feel real or meaningful. The pattern enters a familiar personal item. Follow how the visitor relates this use to the source, its maker, its materials and their own life. [1] [2]

A recognisable relationship

The visitor may value a repeated line, gap or overlap. Ask which feature links the preview to the source. Appearance gives one kind of reason.

A material action

A fold can give a pattern a personal use or make a relationship visible. The original textile and personal item each retain their own material history. Ask how the action relates them.

Maker and context

Someone may refer to the artist’s choices or the work’s background. Record checked context and personal association separately.

A place in my life

The visitor may imagine carrying or wearing the adaptation. Ask what this changes in their relationship with the source. Ownership, authorship and heritage connection can develop in different ways.

Use these as starting points for analysis. Begin with open questions and follow the reasons the visitor introduces.

A worked example

Invented example. A visitor selects parallel lines in the source. G1 merges them on the cuff. They fold the cuff to make an edge clearer and request G2, then explain: “Those two lines connect it to the work for me. I want to keep them when I wear it.” Read this account beside S, C1, G1, the action, C2 and G2.

An explanation about a favourite colour concerns preference. An ignored fold concerns control. A reason tied to the chosen source concerns the heritage connection. Keep these accounts distinct.

Build and pilot

Capture: approve the small window, crop before AI input and storage, and keep the same item across turns. Record whether the input is a personal item, study item, prepared photograph or outline.

Generation: keep the source, prompt template, model version, size and strength steady. Use a fixed seed when supported. Save the original crop and actual generated preview separately. Log random differences, structural errors, timing and assistance.

First check: establish that a clear local action gives a response a visitor can understand and guide. Then examine the source-related reasons. Start with 4–6 usability walkthroughs as a planning estimate and use the observations to plan the research sample.

Theory and the HCI problem

Connections between people and objects

Relational authenticity asks how a connection becomes meaningful through objects, people, making and history. We follow the connections visitors themselves describe. [1]

Looking alike and having a real link

A similar shape is one possible reason. A known maker, source or history gives another kind of link. The personal item and original work each retain their own materials and histories. [2]

Action and response

We examine whether a visitor can see how their material action affects AI, guide the next image and decide when the result no longer fits the source. Embodied generation already has published precedents. [22] [29] [30]

What could be new

The candidate contribution is an account of how visitors maintain or change their grounds for a connection to one named heritage object during material interaction with AI. The interaction technique has close precedents. A contribution would need evidence from the action sequence and the visitor’s reasons. [1] [2] [6] [7] [12] [22] [29] [30]

Input, data and analysis

What we record

Save source S and its selected detail, item type and capture route, approved crops C1 and C2, actual previews G1 and G2, the action between them, pointed regions, brief reasons and keep or stop decisions. Record model settings, timing, help and failures. Keep future-scenario answers as a separate part of the session.

How we read the data

Follow S → C1 → G1 → action → C2 → G2 → reason. Read how the visitor relates a change to the source and the personal item. Analyse visual preference, useful control, authorship and source connection as separate judgments. Include choices to keep, stop or use another input route.

What would make us revise it

First establish that visitors can see a useful relationship between their local action and the next preview. Then examine whether their reasons refer to the source work, its context, the item or personal experience. Use pilot observations to improve structural guidance and reflection prompts.

Set-up, participation and the non-AI route

Smallest working set-up

One permitted source detail and fact card, a fixed camera, a small tabletop window, one screen and one image-conditioned generation service. Visitors choose a cuff or bag corner. A plain study cuff or bag panel provides an alternative. Begin with two deliberate captures.

Lowering entry barriers

Offer seated access, a plain study item and a helper who follows the visitor’s directions. People can point, speak or select a prepared outline. Explain and record the chosen capture route. AI use remains a choice.

If someone declines AI

Compare the source with the selected local item, try a fold or position and keep a source-labelled local-item record. Record the visitor’s choice and reason. This route contributes accounts of participation and source connection.

DIRECTION B · Combines Probes 4 and 5

One Object, Two Interpretations

Start a postcard on a cuff or bag corner, exchange it with a real visitor across languages, then clarify and keep the version that grows from the conversation.

Cross-language exchange6–10 minutes per turn, plus an optional postcard · estimateOptional AI
B

The question

How do visitors retain and negotiate their understanding of a shared heritage object in AI-assisted cross-language exchanges?

The postcard is the shared object of the conversation from the first draft. One person expresses a connection between a heritage detail and a personal item. A real recipient responds to that draft. The sender clarifies and updates the same card, which becomes the take-away.

AI’s role

A language and image assistant around one developing postcard. It translates the visitor’s checked sentence and proposes a pattern adaptation on the approved cuff or bag crop. After the real reply, it can help express a clarification and update that same preview. Each person’s voice stays identified.

What a visitor does

  1. Choose the source and local item

    Use the same named work, numbered detail and fact card. Choose a cuff or bag corner and approve one local crop C in the shared capture window. A prepared item or outline is available.

  2. Say what you want to express

    In your own language, say one short sentence linking the source detail with the item or an association. Check the words before AI uses them.

  3. Build and share draft P1

    AI translates the sentence and proposes a pattern preview for C. A fixed layout keeps the source, original words, translation and AI preview together. Approve what the real recipient may see.

  4. Receive a real reply

    The recipient sees P1 and the shared source. They point to a detail, explain their understanding or ask a question. The reply can come from an available partner or a consented earlier exchange.

  5. Clarify the same postcard

    Accept the reply, clarify the meaning or keep different readings. Update the words on the same card and request one revised image if useful. Keep P1, the reply and the final version P2 linked.

  6. Keep the conversation’s result

    Preview P2 with the chosen words, source credit and AI label. Discuss the future display rule, then choose download or optional one-off email. A card awaiting a real reply is labelled Draft awaiting reply.

One postcard carries the whole conversation

The postcard begins as a draft another visitor can respond to. Link a source detail with your cuff or bag corner and say what you want to express. AI helps picture and translate the idea. A real recipient replies to the draft. Clarify that same card, then keep the version that grows from the conversation.

BEGIN THE CARD

Source + local item + my meaning

Use the same capture window as A. Choose a cuff, bag corner or prepared study item. Say a sentence in your own language, such as “I want this line at my bag’s edge because it reminds me of a path.” Check the words.

AI: translates that sentence and proposes a local pattern preview. A fixed layout adds source credit, exact words and identified translation.

SHARE P1

A real person reads my draft

Approve what the recipient may see. They receive the source detail, words, translation and local-item preview together.

Recipient: identifies a feature, explains their reading or asks a question. Their reply supplies evidence of what the draft conveyed. [8] [9]

CLARIFY THE SAME CARD

The reply shapes P2

Confirm, clarify or keep a different reading. AI can translate the clarification. Request one revised pattern preview if useful.

Record: keep P1, the actual reply, the clarification and final P2 under one card ID. Each word or image change has a place in the conversation.

KEEP ITS RESULT

The final card is the take-away

Select the final words and image. An agreed excerpt of the real reply can appear with its contributor identified. Preview P2 and choose download or optional one-off email.

A card awaiting a real reply keeps that status visible.

What stays on the developing card

Four clear parts: unchanged source thumbnail and credit, local-item pattern preview, checked original words with identified translation, and draft or final status. The final card can retain an agreed reply excerpt. A fixed layout preserves words and credits as the image changes.

A personal association remains labelled as the visitor’s response. “A path” may be what a repeated line means to that person. The source’s documented background stays alongside it. A plain card carries the same source, local-item reference and words through the real exchange.

GIFT and museum data souvenirs provide precedents for object-related personal messages and take-aways. B brings the take-away into the conversation from its first draft. The card people keep is also the object through which they understood one another. [15] [26]

Follow how meaning becomes shared

Authenticity enters when a visitor decides whether the card expresses a meaningful connection to the named work, and another person reads and responds to it. Follow the intended meaning, what P1 conveys and what clarification changes. [1] [3] [16]

Keep source facts, translation fit, recipient understanding and author approval as separate observations. Two people may understand each other and keep different readings. Follow those outcomes across P1, the reply and P2.

The shared station supports two different interactions. A moves forward through the next fold or position. B moves forward through a real reply and a clarification on the same card. One approved local crop can remain fixed throughout B.

  2. Request delivery. Enter and confirm your own address in the private receipt step.
  3. Use the study’s private service. The server sends the approved image, PDF or private expiring link and shows queued, sent or failed. Download stays available.
  4. Separate the records. A session code links research material. A restricted delivery store holds the address and job status for the agreed support period. AI receives the checked design inputs.

Data and first-trial questions

Prompts: “Which part did the other person respond to?” “What did you want them to understand?” “What changed after their reply?” “How does the final card connect to the source and your own item?”

Pilot the whole journey: use one draft image and one optional revision. Link asynchronous turns by card ID and show reply status. Keep capture and sharing choices visible. Receipt choice and delivery status stay separate from the research explanation.

Theory and the HCI problem

Relational authenticity

Follow how the object, a person’s experience and the other visitor become connected through exchange. [1] [16]

Personal voice and recognition

Separate the sender’s endorsement of wording, the recipient’s understanding, historical accuracy and the recognition of an interpretation. [3] [17]

Agency and cultural expression

Being able to change a translation can help people express themselves. It can also make a conversation take more effort. AI writing suggestions may remove cultural details. These findings help us choose what to observe. [8] [9] [10]

What could be new

The candidate contribution is an account of how a source-related personal meaning develops through a multilingual conversation around one AI-assisted visual postcard. Its draft and revision show how people recognise, question and clarify each other’s interpretations. The final card keeps the outcome of that same process. [1] [3] [8] [9] [10] [16] [17] [25] [26]

Input, data and analysis

What we record

Follow one card ID through source S, local crop C and its sharing choice, initial words M1, identified translation, actual draft P1, real reply R, clarification M2 and final P2. Retain accepted and declined image changes. Delivery choice and job status use a separate restricted record.

How we read the data

Follow how a particular source detail and meaning travel through the first draft, the recipient’s response and the sender’s clarification. Examine words and images together. Distinguish source accuracy, translation fit, recipient understanding, author approval and personal connection.

What would make us revise it

Check that a recipient replies to the actual source-linked draft. Follow whether the sender’s clarification changes the words, image or their stated connection to the work. Keep pending replies visible as pending. Test the full draft–reply–clarification–receipt journey.

01 · Ask About a Detail

Choose part of an object image. Say what you notice. Ask AI one question, then say what you think after its answer.

We collect: the chosen detail, question, actual answer and any change in the visitor’s explanation.

After an AI answer, what does a visitor use to decide what to believe about an object?

We look for reasons, such as a visible detail, a museum source or personal knowledge. We also ask when the person checks or rejects the answer.

A connection to an object can come from its history, appearance or a personal memory. These are different reasons. We keep them separate when reading the visitor’s account. This uses relational authenticity and the difference between looking alike and having a real source link. [1] [2]The book case. The Statistical Clock

The Statistical Clock is an imagined household clock that reports deaths in different kinds of transport. It takes information people may avoid and puts it into an ordinary daily object. The designers ask what kind of life would make someone want this clock. The clock helps people discuss that life.

The idea we borrow. Put a familiar rule into a small, visible object so people can discuss how that rule shapes daily life.

How it connects to this probe. The clock makes daily reminders visible. Probe 1 can make the rule “AI speaks first” visible through a small museum display card. The question then concerns how that rule shapes the start of a visit.

The step it changes. After the normal question task, place the AI answer at the top of a clearly imagined museum display card. Put the visitor’s own explanation beside it. Ask what changes when the AI version always goes first.

How this helps us choose. Keep 1 as a short introduction. A future standalone version could focus on who sets the starting point of interpretation.

03 · Change One Detail

Select an area of an image, tell AI what may change, then compare the changed version with the source.

We collect: selected area, instruction, actual image and reasons for keeping or rejecting it.

Which changes still feel connected to the object, and which changes lose that connection?

We need to hear why a change matters. Ask what the preferred picture carries from the source. The HCI issue is how people set and check limits for generation.

Troika’s Plant Fiction presents invented plants with plausible images and short texts about how they might be used in a near-future London. The plants belong to a made-up situation. The project tells viewers that it is fiction, so they can explore the situation without being told a false discovery has occurred. One example, Selfeater, is imagined to break down its own material to help make fuel. Its picture and short story let viewers think about that future use.

01 / MAKING

Huayao embroidery: follow a change and its reason

The original study

Eight experienced Huayao embroiderers worked through a six-month, four-stage process. It included 152 embroidery images, three generation workflows, selecting images and making embroidery. The final in-situ stages lasted eight days. Researchers used interviews during making, a focus group and open coding. The publication is a CHI 2026 extended abstract. [12]

What the researchers observed

Participants selected, discarded and changed generated images. Their reasons included familiar local motifs, animal forms, line density and what would work in embroidery. The authors describe how making helped bring further judgments into view.

Dianhua: “I still have to change it a bit”. The authors’ discussion: “many of the modifications made during the making offer insights”. [12]

The step this changes in A

Keep the actual change and the reason together. A records the chosen source, local crop C1, preview G1, the cuff or bag adjustment, crop C2 and preview G2. The short replay asks why a particular change mattered. Experienced embroidery and a brief visitor activity offer different settings for this way of examining making.

The study informs our attention to reasons that develop through practice. A’s visitor task investigates how those reasons relate a named work to a personal item. See the authenticity guide and the Art Morph comparison. [1] [2] [12]

The AI Pattern Merchant: place a working design in a social setting

What happened

The project brought prompting, generation and printing into a Huayao festival and the relationships of making and exchange. The authors report attention to the inserted samples and record that none were purchased. [11]

A working object gives people something concrete to encounter and discuss. A’s local-item preview and B’s developing postcard can carry a wider future-museum question. We introduce that future label clearly after the ordinary interaction.

05 / SPECULATION

The original words and our design reasoning

Speculation means making an imagined situation clear enough to try and discuss. Here we ask how a future museum could work differently, then use the activity to help people judge that possibility.

For A

A future museum shares changing patterns for personal cuffs and bags. Two actual previews and a fictional label help visitors discuss how these versions carry a source work forward.

For B

A future museum shows postcards made through visitor conversations. The draft, reply and final version help people decide what a later visitor should see.

Sequence matters. Complete the ordinary activity first. Clearly introduce the future scenario afterwards. Separate current usability feedback from judgments about the proposed arrangement. Record how people discuss the proposed future and the ordinary activity as two parts of the session.

The thirteen passages below combine seven method passages, a selection principle and five case-linked passages. All are original sentences from Anthony Dunne and Fiona Raby’s Speculative Everything (2013). Pages are printed book pages, checked against the supplied pages. Footnote markers are omitted. The meanings and applications are our interpretations. The museum activities are our design proposals. The book cases help explain their development. The case images below are credited screenshots of the book figures. [21]

How selection uses speculation. Start with the book case, name the idea we borrow, and show the exact activity step it changes. A uses its local-item previews to discuss how a museum work can enter personal life. B uses one developing postcard to discuss how meanings are shared and displayed. These links help explain the selection.

Passage 1 · pp. 2–3Use possible futures to understand the present

What we are interested in, though, is the idea of possible futures and using them as tools to better understand the present and to discuss the kind of future people want, and, of course, ones people do not want.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 1, pp. 2–3. [21]

The idea we take from it

A possible future helps people examine present choices and discuss how they want to live.

How it changes the activity

A imagines a museum work continuing through personal-item patterns. B imagines a museum displaying postcards that grow through conversations. The possible futures help visitors examine their own interaction.

Passage 2 · pp. 3A future is a medium for thinking

For us futures are not a destination or something to be strived for but a medium to aid imaginative thought—to speculate with.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 1, pp. 3. [21]

The idea we take from it

A possible future lets people think about a way of living and decide what they want to keep or change.

How it changes the activity

After the ordinary activity, introduce a future museum arrangement. Ask what it would change and what visitors would keep, alter or refuse. This discussion connects the small AI task to choices about museum life.

Passage 3 · pp. 90A prop makes another possibility discussable

They help us think about alternative possibilities—they challenge the ideals, values, and beliefs of our society embodied in material culture.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 6, pp. 90. [21]

The idea we take from it

The object supports reflection on the values embodied in a possible arrangement.

How it changes the activity

A uses its local-item previews and a future label. B uses the draft, reply and final postcard. Each small prop gives people something concrete to point to when they discuss a museum’s choices.

Passage 4 · pp. 92A prop can actually work

For some, the term prop means a fake object, something that doesn’t work, but in the context of a speculative design, a prop can be a fully working prototype or not.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 6, pp. 92. [21]

The idea we take from it

A working prototype can serve as a prop for an imagined situation. Its role is to make that situation available for discussion.

How it changes the activity

Run the actual image and translation functions during the study. Use their outputs as props for the later, clearly introduced future scenario.

Passage 5 · pp. 93Leave space for people’s own interpretations

Prefigurative futures (or pre-enactments?) also use props to transport viewers’ imagination but, whereas in this role they are bringing a specific future to the present so that it can be partially experienced in advance, user-tested, or prepared for, we are more interested in using props to transport viewers’ imagination into a thought experiment, or what-if, and allow enough room for them to make their own interpretations.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 6, pp. 93. [21]

The idea we take from it

Participants can give their own answers to an imagined situation. The researcher should leave those answers open.

How it changes the activity

Visitors can accept, alter or stop an AI preview. They can keep different interpretations after understanding one another. Their choices shape the discussion.

Passage 6 · pp. 161People may make their own judgments

We view people as free agents, not necessarily rational, but free to make up their own minds.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 9, pp. 161. [21]

The idea we take from it

This is a commitment to participants’ freedom to judge and choose.

How it changes the activity

People can choose the AI route, a prepared input, a plain card and their receipt method. Pilot sessions reveal which routes need further support.

Passage 7 · pp. 2Create a space for discussion

This form of design thrives on imagination and aims to open up new perspectives on what are sometimes called wicked problems, to create spaces for discussion and debate about alternative ways of being, and to inspire and encourage people’s imaginations to flow freely.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 1, pp. 2. [21]

The idea we take from it

Speculation opens a discussion about how life could be different. A feature test needs this wider question to become part of that discussion.

How it changes the activity

A discusses how personal use carries a work forward. B discusses how a museum can show a meaning that developed between people. Both invite alternative answers.

Passage 8 · pp. 35An alternative must open a gap with the present

It is the gap between reality as we know it and the different idea of reality referred to in the critical design proposal that creates the space for discussion.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 3, pp. 35. [21]

The idea we take from it

A proposal opens discussion by making another arrangement available. Its fictional rule gives participants a concrete alternative to compare with their present experience.

How it changes the activity

For each probe, explain the current activity, the future museum rule, the prop that makes it visible and the choice left to participants. This sequence explains why we select and revise a direction.

Dunne & Raby with Michael Anastassiades. The Statistical Clock is an imagined household clock that reports deaths in different kinds of transport.
