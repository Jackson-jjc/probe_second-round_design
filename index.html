The wider research question and what “authenticity” means here

How do human–AI interactions shape visitors’ experiences of authenticity in museums and heritage sites?

The two supporting questions ask what visitors draw on for authenticity, and how they negotiate different senses of authenticity when interpreting heritage objects with AI. A and B are focused parts of this larger project.

We examine historical accuracy, resemblance, felt connection, personal voice and another person’s understanding as distinct judgments. The activities show how they relate. The theories help us decide what to notice. Visitors can give their own reasons and feelings. [1] [2] [3] [17]

01 / THE CURRENT FIRST-STAGE TEST

Try A and B, then help choose the next direction

Protocol basis: Probe Development Survey, ERGO 117392, V1.0, 21 October 2026. Recruitment begins after ethics approval.

10–15 adults

ECS staff or students aged 18 or over. No museum or AI expertise is required.

10–15 minutes

Try A, then B, about three minutes each. Complete four optional questionnaire questions.

Prepared AI examples

The researcher checks AI content before sessions and manually switches examples or assembles cards during the test.

One postcard wall

Anonymous A and B cards join a local study wall. Optional paper copies contain only the participant’s own card.

Purpose: compare the two low-fidelity prototypes, identify useful improvements and choose which direction to develop further. The website explains the research designs. Participants try the prototypes on study equipment. Museum fieldwork is a later development stage.

The session, in order

  1. Read the information sheet, ask questions and tick consent.
  2. Try A: choose a source detail, arrange materials and compare prepared cuff/bag effects.
  3. Try B: write original words, choose a prepared image, then read, reply or clarify on a postcard.
  4. Complete the four optional questions.
  5. Check drafts for identifying details and confirm submission. Both cards join the wall.
  6. Choose whether to take printed copies of your own cards, without others’ replies.

What we keep

  • Consent tick and questionnaire answers.
  • A: selected source-detail and prepared-image references.
  • B: original words, replies, clarifications, source/image references and reply links.

Keep card text and references to existing images. Submitted cards are visible to later participants. Questionnaire answers remain with the research team and authorised audit staff.

How the test is recorded

Data come from the submitted questionnaire and postcard text and references. A’s physical arrangements are not retained. The study excludes names, signatures, contacts, demographics, photographs, screenshots, audio, video, observation notes and interaction logs.

AI content is prepared before the session. Participant writing is kept out of AI services. The researcher openly explains the manual simulation.

The four questionnaire questions
  1. Which prototype should we develop further for a museum experience? Tick one.
  2. A: As you arranged materials and compared the source with AI cuff/bag previews, what kept or changed what felt authentic? Why?
  3. B: How did your words, the AI image and others' replies affect what you expressed or understood across cultural perspectives?
  4. Why did you choose that option, and what would you improve, including the postcard wall or take-away?

Question 1 options: A, B, Both equally, Neither, Not sure.

How the feedback informs the next design

Summarise preferences and reasons, then themes about authenticity, cultural expression, understanding, AI images and improvements. Read B replies alongside the relevant card. Exclude labelled AI starters from participant evidence. Use the findings to choose and refine a direction.

This first stage gathers responses to low-fidelity examples. A later working-AI study can specify how to capture moment-by-moment interaction and evaluate live generation or translation.

Participation and storage

Participants can skip activities or questions, stop, or discard drafts before submission. After anonymous submission, cards and forms cannot be located for removal. Check the cards for identifying details before submission.

The wall runs locally on study equipment without accounts or public access. Lock paper forms and use University-approved secure UK digital storage. Retain data for at least 10 years after collection or publication, whichever is later, then review disposal.

01 / MAKING

Huayao embroidery: follow a change and its reason

The original study

Eight experienced Huayao embroiderers worked through a six-month, four-stage process. It included 152 embroidery images, three generation workflows, selecting images and making embroidery. The final in-situ stages lasted eight days. Researchers used interviews during making, a focus group and open coding. The publication is a CHI 2026 extended abstract. [12]

What the researchers observed

Participants selected, discarded and changed generated images. Their reasons included familiar local motifs, animal forms, line density and what would work in embroidery. The authors describe how making helped bring further judgments into view.

Dianhua: “I still have to change it a bit”. The authors’ discussion: “many of the modifications made during the making offer insights”. [12]

The step this changes in A

Connect the source, example and participant’s written reason clearly. In the current A, participants arrange materials and compare prepared effects. Question 2 asks which source connections kept or changed what felt authentic. Selected-example references provide context for that written reflection. Experienced embroidery and a brief visitor activity offer different settings for this way of examining making.

The study informs our attention to reasons that develop through practice. A’s visitor task investigates how those reasons relate a named work to a personal item. See the authenticity guide and the Art Morph comparison. [1] [2] [12]

Let people clarify in their own language

What the study found

A study of 45 dyads of Chinese new immigrants and English-speaking residents in the US examined translation interfaces. Post-editing supported greater exercise of agency and added costs to the conversation. [9]

The step this changes in B

B keeps the participant’s original words and supports replies and clarification on a postcard. Prepared multilingual starter notes introduce the wider translation idea. Current participant text stays outside AI services. Live translation is a later development possibility.

Research on cultural detail in AI writing and colour-name translation also helps us look closely at what a fluent version carries. [8] [10]

Useful museum and HCI precedents

Cleveland Museum of Art

Art Morph connects materials with generated art. Endless Threads combines a camera cutout with a chosen textile using OpenCV. These offer two forms for linking a person with a collection image. [22] [29]

GIFT and personal records

GIFT connects objects with personal messages. Museum data souvenirs connect a visit with a personal take-away. B develops that take-away inside the conversation. [15] [16] [26]

Embodied generation

Body-prompting research and A(I)Cam show ways to make generation an embodied public or reflective experience. A borrows the idea of material exploration. Its first-stage test uses prepared everyday-item effects and written reflections. [30] [31]

Other research and reading scope

AI co-writing informs the distinction between a writer’s approval and a reader’s understanding. Heritage narratives, TwistLens and Gen-Diaolou add context for interpretation and transformation. Nasher provides an AI curatorial-text precedent. [3] [4] [5] [14] [25]

ZOMA is used through its abstract as a hybrid-craft precedent. The empathy paper is also used at abstract level as a prompt to consider attribution. Source entries identify the material read and the study setting. [7] [20]

06 / SPECULATIVE DESIGN REASONING

The original words and the ideas they give us

The book cases explain how the probes were developed and suggest possible future museum arrangements. The current first-stage procedures and questions are listed in the test section.

A possible future for A

A museum shares adaptations for everyday cuffs and bags. A small postcard can help people imagine how a source pattern enters personal life.

A possible future for B

A museum shows visitor interpretations together with the replies that shaped them. A postcard wall makes questions about visibility and voice concrete.

How this enters the current design. Use clear source-to-example links in A and keep original words with the chosen image in B. The protocol gathers feedback through the two activities, postcards and four questionnaire questions. Further future-scenario interviews are a later design possibility.

Thirteen original passages from Anthony Dunne and Fiona Raby’s Speculative Everything (2013) are preserved below with printed page numbers. Interpretations and design applications are ours. Book figures keep their original captions and credits. [21]

Passage 1 · pp. 2–3Use possible futures to understand the present

What we are interested in, though, is the idea of possible futures and using them as tools to better understand the present and to discuss the kind of future people want, and, of course, ones people do not want.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 1, pp. 2–3. [21]

The idea we take from it

A possible future helps people examine present choices and discuss how they want to live.

How it informs the design

A’s prepared everyday-item examples and B’s postcard wall make possible museum arrangements concrete. The first-stage questionnaire helps refine these directions.

Passage 2 · pp. 3A future is a medium for thinking

For us futures are not a destination or something to be strived for but a medium to aid imaginative thought—to speculate with.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 1, pp. 3. [21]

The idea we take from it

A possible future lets people think about a way of living and decide what they want to keep or change.

How it informs the design

Use the imagined museum as a design tool. The current test gathers feedback through the submitted activities and four questionnaire questions. A separate future-scenario interview can be planned later.

Passage 3 · pp. 90A prop makes another possibility discussable

They help us think about alternative possibilities—they challenge the ideals, values, and beliefs of our society embodied in material culture.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 6, pp. 90. [21]

The idea we take from it

The object supports reflection on the values embodied in a possible arrangement.

How it informs the design

A uses its chosen postcard and B uses its words, image and replies as concrete design props. These help us think about the museum arrangements described above.

Passage 4 · pp. 92A prop can actually work

For some, the term prop means a fake object, something that doesn’t work, but in the context of a speculative design, a prop can be a fully working prototype or not.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 6, pp. 92. [21]

The idea we take from it

A working prototype can serve as a prop for an imagined situation. Its role is to make that situation available for discussion.

How it informs the design

A future working AI system could also serve as a speculative prop. The first-stage study uses openly explained manual simulation and prepared AI content.

Passage 5 · pp. 93Leave space for people’s own interpretations

Prefigurative futures (or pre-enactments?) also use props to transport viewers’ imagination but, whereas in this role they are bringing a specific future to the present so that it can be partially experienced in advance, user-tested, or prepared for, we are more interested in using props to transport viewers’ imagination into a thought experiment, or what-if, and allow enough room for them to make their own interpretations.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 6, pp. 93. [21]

The idea we take from it

Participants can give their own answers to an imagined situation. The researcher should leave those answers open.

How it informs the design

Let participants choose, clarify, disagree, skip or discard drafts before submitting. Their own interpretations can guide development.

Passage 6 · pp. 161People may make their own judgments

We view people as free agents, not necessarily rational, but free to make up their own minds.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 9, pp. 161. [21]

The idea we take from it

This is a commitment to participants’ freedom to judge and choose.

How it informs the design

Make participation voluntary. The current study allows people to skip activities or questions and choose whether to keep a printed copy of their own card.

Passage 7 · pp. 2Create a space for discussion

This form of design thrives on imagination and aims to open up new perspectives on what are sometimes called wicked problems, to create spaces for discussion and debate about alternative ways of being, and to inspire and encourage people’s imaginations to flow freely.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 1, pp. 2. [21]

The idea we take from it

Speculation opens a discussion about how life could be different. A feature test needs this wider question to become part of that discussion.

How it informs the design

A wider museum discussion could grow from these two prototypes. The current study first collects development preferences, authenticity reflections and cultural-expression feedback.

Passage 8 · pp. 35An alternative must open a gap with the present

It is the gap between reality as we know it and the different idea of reality referred to in the critical design proposal that creates the space for discussion.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 3, pp. 35. [21]

The idea we take from it

A proposal opens discussion by making another arrangement available. Its fictional rule gives participants a concrete alternative to compare with their present experience.

How it informs the design

Explain the present activity, the imagined museum arrangement and the concrete object that makes it understandable. Keep this design reasoning distinct from the first-stage questionnaire.

Passage 9 · pp. 35–36The Statistical Clock · reasoning for Probe 01

Dunne & Raby with Michael Anastassiades. The Statistical Clock is an imagined household clock that reports deaths in different kinds of transport. It takes information people may avoid and puts it into an ordinary daily object. The designers ask what kind of life would make someone want this clock. The clock helps people discuss that life.

What would have to change for a need like this to emerge?

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 3, pp. 35–36. [21]

The idea we take from it

Put a familiar rule into a small, visible object so people can discuss how that rule shapes daily life. The clock turns a daily information rule into a visible object. Probe 1 similarly suggests making the order of AI and visitor explanations visible. This remains a possible future variant.

How it informs the design

A later Probe 1 could compare a fictional display that puts AI first with one that puts the visitor’s words first. The current first-stage test concentrates on A and B. Keep 1 as a short introduction. A future standalone version could focus on who sets the starting point of interpretation.

Passage 10 · pp. 92–93The Islanders · reasoning for Probe 02

Charles Avery, discussed by Dunne & Raby. In Charles Avery’s The Islanders, drawings, objects, models and found bones appear to come from a traveller’s visit to an invented island. Viewers use these small clues to imagine a larger place and its way of life. A small physical thing can therefore help someone think about an imagined world.

It is the emphasis on transporting the imagination that distinguishes them from other object types including products, prototypes, and models.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 6, pp. 92–93. [21]

The idea we take from it

A small object can help people imagine the larger world in which it belongs. The island’s objects suggest a larger world. A’s prepared cuff/bag examples and postcard suggest an everyday setting where a heritage pattern travels into someone’s life.

How it informs the design

In first-stage A, connect the source detail, sample arrangement and everyday-item effect clearly. Question 2 asks what kept or changed what felt authentic. A fictional future museum label remains a later design possibility. Develop A as material exploration with prepared everyday-item adaptations. This first-stage study asks for written authenticity reflections and selected-example references. Later work can examine a working AI response.

Passage 11 · pp. 94–95Plant Fiction · reasoning for Probe 03

Troika, discussed by Dunne & Raby. Troika’s Plant Fiction presents invented plants with plausible images and short texts about how they might be used in a near-future London. The plants belong to a made-up situation. The project tells viewers that it is fiction, so they can explore the situation without being told a false discovery has occurred. One example, Selfeater, is imagined to break down its own material to help make fuel. Its picture and short story let viewers think about that future use.

There is a very important difference between inviting viewers to “make-believe” and asking them to “believe.”

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 6, pp. 94–95. [21]

The idea we take from it

Give an imagined image a clear setting and purpose, so viewers can explore the proposed future. Plant Fiction gives its imagined plants a clear use. A’s cuff or bag picture gives a pattern adaptation a similarly concrete everyday use.

How it informs the design

Show the source alongside the prepared everyday-item effect in A. Let the participant compare examples and select a postcard. This gives the image-change idea a purpose within A. Keep the visual-change idea inside A. A separate Probe 3 would need a distinct purpose or research question.

Passage 12 · pp. 173–174United Micro Kingdoms · reasoning for Probe 04

Dunne & Raby. United Micro Kingdoms imagines several different societies and designs a vehicle for each one. Each vehicle shows choices about how people travel and live together. Looking at them helps viewers compare what each society makes easier and what it gives up. The pictured Digicars belong to a made-up society where software manages travel and road prices. The Biocar belongs to a society built around biotechnology. These two images show how different choices can shape everyday travel.

Each vehicle would embody different ideologies, values, priorities, and belief systems—essentially alternative worldviews.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 9, pp. 173–174. [21]

The idea we take from it

Make different choices visible through objects people can compare. The vehicles embody choices about shared life. A postcard wall similarly makes a choice about whose interpretations become visible. B keeps original words and replies together.

How it informs the design

In first-stage B, keep the source, original text and chosen image on one postcard. Let later participants read submitted cards and reply. Further discussion of a future museum display belongs to later development. Combine 4 and 5 as B. A single local postcard carries original words, a prepared image and replies. It also becomes a take-away copy.

Passage 13 · pp. 40–42Huggable Atomic Mushrooms · reasoning for Probe 05

Dunne & Raby with Michael Anastassiades. Huggable Atomic Mushrooms are soft objects shaped like clouds from nuclear tests. Their friendly feel sits beside a frightening subject. This makes viewers ask why an inviting object presents that subject in this way. Their meaning comes from this relationship between form and subject.

It is through its demeanor that one starts to wonder just how serious it is.

Dunne & Raby (2013), Chapter 3, pp. 40–42. [21]

The idea we take from it

The way an object looks and feels can change how its subject is understood. The soft mushroom form affects how viewers encounter its serious subject. B similarly lets us ask how a friendly AI image changes the tone of a person’s interpretation.

How it informs the design

Let participants choose a prepared image to accompany their exact words. Read replies and questionnaire Question 3 to understand what that combination conveyed. Combine 5 with 4. Expression, replies and an optional own-card copy form one simple activity.
