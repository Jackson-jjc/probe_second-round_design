# Speculation · Original passages and design reasoning

Dunne and Raby (2013), Speculative Everything [21]. Printed book pages. Footnote markers omitted. Interpretations and applications are ours. A working AI, a card or an image edit is not automatically speculative.

## Passage 1 · Use possible futures to understand the present



> What we are interested in, though, is the idea of possible futures and using them as tools to better understand the present and to discuss the kind of future people want, and, of course, ones people do not want.

Chapter 1, pp. 2–3.

**What this supports.** A possible future helps people examine present choices. It is not a prediction.

**Our use and its limit.** A asks who may record a visitor’s experience. B asks whose interpretations a museum should retain. These proposed arrangements are ours, rather than examples from the book.

## Passage 2 · A future is a medium for thinking



> For us futures are not a destination or something to be strived for but a medium to aid imaginative thought—to speculate with.

Chapter 1, pp. 3.

**What this supports.** The purpose is to think through an arrangement, rather than demonstrate the preferred future.

**Our use and its limit.** After the ordinary activity, explicitly introduce a future museum arrangement. Ask what it would change, what visitors would keep, and what they would refuse. A routine AI task alone is not enough.

## Passage 3 · A prop makes another possibility discussable



> They help us think about alternative possibilities—they challenge the ideals, values, and beliefs of our society embodied in material culture.

Chapter 6, pp. 90.

**What this supports.** The object supports reflection on the values embodied in a possible arrangement.

**Our use and its limit.** A’s experience card makes a different way of recording a visit visible. B’s parallel conversation card makes a different way of retaining interpretations visible. A souvenir or image edit alone does not establish this function.

## Passage 4 · A prop can actually work



> For some, the term prop means a fake object, something that doesn’t work, but in the context of a speculative design, a prop can be a fully working prototype or not.

Chapter 6, pp. 92.

**What this supports.** Working technology is compatible with a speculative prop. Functionality is not the deciding criterion.

**Our use and its limit.** Run the actual AI in A and B, retain its real outputs and visitor changes, and use the resulting card to discuss the alternative arrangement. This does not make every working AI prototype speculative.

## Passage 5 · Leave space for people’s own interpretations



> Prefigurative futures (or pre-enactments?) also use props to transport viewers’ imagination but, whereas in this role they are bringing a specific future to the present so that it can be partially experienced in advance, user-tested, or prepared for, we are more interested in using props to transport viewers’ imagination into a thought experiment, or what-if, and allow enough room for them to make their own interpretations.

Chapter 6, pp. 93.

**What this supports.** Participants should interpret a thought experiment rather than rehearse a future already decided by the researcher.

**Our use and its limit.** A permits approval, correction, rejection or indifference. B permits disagreement after mutual understanding. Do not require visitors to conclude that AI damages authenticity. Applying the prop idea to digital cards is our methodological transfer.

## Passage 6 · People may make their own judgments



> We view people as free agents, not necessarily rational, but free to make up their own minds.

Chapter 9, pp. 161.

**What this supports.** This is a commitment to participants’ freedom to judge and choose.

**Our use and its limit.** Both directions allow a non-AI route. Visitors can reject wording or reject the future arrangement. Receiving a card does not depend on using AI. This principle does not establish universal accessibility or a causal comparison between self-selected routes.

## Passage 7 · Create a space for discussion



> This form of design thrives on imagination and aims to open up new perspectives on what are sometimes called wicked problems, to create spaces for discussion and debate about alternative ways of being, and to inspire and encourage people’s imaginations to flow freely.

Chapter 1, pp. 2.

**What this supports.** Speculation opens discussion of alternatives rather than merely evaluating a product feature.

**Our use and its limit.** B asks whether different interpretations should remain side by side and who decides what is kept. A asks whether an AI account can represent a lived experience. The book does not itself propose AI museum cards or multilingual labels.

## Passage 8 · An alternative must open a gap with the present



> It is the gap between reality as we know it and the different idea of reality referred to in the critical design proposal that creates the space for discussion.

Chapter 3, pp. 35.

**What this supports.** A proposal opens discussion by making another arrangement available. Improving a feature within an unchanged arrangement is not sufficient on its own.

**Our use and its limit.** For each probe, identify the present default, the changed rule, the prop that makes it visible and the choice left to participants. Use this to explain selection and revision, not to score the probes with a universal scale.

## Passage 9 · The Statistical Clock · reasoning for Probe 01

Dunne & Raby with Michael Anastassiades. The clock in Do You Want to Replace the Existing Normal? reports fatalities by transport category. The authors imagine demand for an everyday object that reminds people of mortality. The question concerns the values and needs of that world, not a novel clock display.

> What would have to change for a need like this to emerge?

Chapter 3, pp. 35–36.

**What this supports.** The current task offers AI answers, but no different museum arrangement. Checking accuracy alone investigates information use rather than a speculative alternative. The book case supplies a methodological comparison, not evidence of the probe’s effectiveness.

**Our use and its limit.** After the ordinary task, show a clearly fictional first-interpretation record with the AI version marked as the default. Ask what a museum would have to value for this rule to make sense and whether the visitor would keep it. Keep 1 as an introduction. An authority-focused revision is possible, but it adds a separate question and is not needed for the first A/B trial. The clock is an analogy for questioning a need or default. It does not prove that AI-first museum interpretation is harmful or unprecedented.

## Passage 10 · The Islanders · reasoning for Probe 02

Charles Avery, discussed by Dunne & Raby. The book describes drawings, objects, models and found bones attributed to a fictional traveller’s observations of an imaginary island. A small object invites viewers to imagine the wider world it belongs to. It is not simply an unusual object to own.

> It is the emphasis on transporting the imagination that distinguishes them from other object types including products, prototypes, and models.

Chapter 6, pp. 92–93.

**What this supports.** Handling cloth, using AI and printing a card are still ordinary activity features. None by itself changes the arrangement through which a visit is represented. The book case supplies a methodological comparison, not evidence of the probe’s effectiveness.

**Our use and its limit.** Use the existing card, clearly labelled as a future-scenario prop, and ask which account would remain if only one were carried forward. The visitor may keep AI wording, replace it or reject the rule. Prioritize A conditionally. The same card already produced by the activity can make a broader recording rule discussable with little extra effort. The speculative question concerns representation, not whether the cloth becomes historical. The Islanders informs how a small prop can suggest a world. It does not validate an AI card or show that visitors will interpret the scenario as intended.

## Passage 11 · Plant Fiction · reasoning for Probe 03

Troika, discussed by Dunne & Raby. Troika’s fictitious plants combine plausible-looking images with short accounts of their roles in a near-future London. The project signals fiction while making a specific environmental arrangement imaginable. It is not presented as a factual plant discovery.

> There is a very important difference between inviting viewers to “make-believe” and asking them to “believe.”

Chapter 6, pp. 94–95.

**What this supports.** Changing a colour or outline supplies a visual alternative, but does not yet identify which heritage value or interpretive arrangement is at stake. The book case supplies a methodological comparison, not evidence of the probe’s effectiveness.

**Our use and its limit.** Compare the approved source and one clearly labelled imagined adaptation. Ask what should remain recognisable and why. Keep historical facts separate, and do not describe the variant as a restoration. Pause the standalone probe. First specify a meaningful adaptation and its contextual stakes. More attractive editing or a printed card does not resolve this gap. Plant Fiction supports framing and legibility of the fiction. It does not make an arbitrary image edit speculative, historically defensible or ethically appropriate.

## Passage 12 · United Micro Kingdoms · reasoning for Probe 04

Dunne & Raby. Different vehicles make alternative arrangements of everyday life and their trade-offs available for comparison. The comparison works because the objects embody different priorities. Four unusual vehicle designs alone would not communicate that argument.

> Each vehicle would embody different ideologies, values, priorities, and belief systems—essentially alternative worldviews.

Chapter 9, pp. 173–174.

**What this supports.** Two languages or two opinions are not yet a different institutional arrangement. A jointly written label may even hide the disagreement the probe needs to investigate. The book case supplies a methodological comparison, not evidence of the probe’s effectiveness.

**Our use and its limit.** Use one existing conversation card plus a fictional display-rule strip. Participants can keep the default, require original and translation together, or refuse the proposed rule. These are discussion choices, not implemented publishing permissions. Merge with 5 as B. 4 supplies the public interpretation question, while 5 adds a real recipient and a chance to correct understanding. A stated visibility rule gives the merged activity a more concrete speculative focus. A difference of language is not a proxy for one of the book’s political systems. The transferable principle is making priorities and trade-offs visible, not classifying visitors into cultural types.

## Passage 13 · Huggable Atomic Mushrooms · reasoning for Probe 05

Dunne & Raby with Michael Anastassiades. The soft, huggable mushroom forms refer to nuclear tests. The book describes a tension between their inviting material presence and what they represent. Their critical force is not equivalent to being cute, soft or amusing.

> It is through its demeanor that one starts to wonder just how serious it is.

Chapter 3, pp. 40–42.

**What this supports.** Gifting or making a message warmer is not automatically speculative. Optional emoji can make an exchange pleasant without raising a question about what that representation retains or changes. The book case supplies a methodological comparison, not evidence of the probe’s effectiveness.

**Our use and its limit.** Keep the original wording, AI wording and a real recipient’s reply on the same card. Discuss a meaningful difference and whether a future museum may retain the reworded version as the default. Do not require a disagreement or introduce a misleading historical claim. Merge with 4. The real recipient makes a representation’s consequence discussable. Keep generated expressions as a later branch, rather than treating visual play as the source of speculation. The book’s deliberately conflicting material form is a methodological analogy. It is not evidence that emoji communicate universally or that heritage messages should be made shocking.
