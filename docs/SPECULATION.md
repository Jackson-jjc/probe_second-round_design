# Speculation · Original passages and design reasoning

Dunne and Raby (2013), Speculative Everything [21]. Printed book pages. Footnote markers omitted. Interpretations and applications are ours. Credited book figure crops appear in the website table and beside the case quotations. A working AI, a card or an image edit is not automatically speculative.

## Passage 1 · Use possible futures to understand the present



> What we are interested in, though, is the idea of possible futures and using them as tools to better understand the present and to discuss the kind of future people want, and, of course, ones people do not want.

Chapter 1, pp. 2–3.

**What this supports.** A possible future helps people examine present choices. It is not a prediction.

**Our use and its limit.** A asks what happens when a museum work becomes a changing visitor-and-AI display. B asks whose words remain visible across languages. These are our proposed museum situations.

## Passage 2 · A future is a medium for thinking



> For us futures are not a destination or something to be strived for but a medium to aid imaginative thought—to speculate with.

Chapter 1, pp. 3.

**What this supports.** A possible future lets people think about a way of living and decide what they want to keep or change.

**Our use and its limit.** After the ordinary activity, explicitly introduce a future museum arrangement. Ask what it would change, what visitors would keep, and what they would refuse. A routine AI task alone is not enough.

## Passage 3 · A prop makes another possibility discussable



> They help us think about alternative possibilities—they challenge the ideals, values, and beliefs of our society embodied in material culture.

Chapter 6, pp. 90.

**What this supports.** The object supports reflection on the values embodied in a possible arrangement.

**Our use and its limit.** A uses the actual layout and generated images beside a fictional future label. B uses a conversation card and display rule. These props help people discuss what the imagined museum changes.

## Passage 4 · A prop can actually work



> For some, the term prop means a fake object, something that doesn’t work, but in the context of a speculative design, a prop can be a fully working prototype or not.

Chapter 6, pp. 92.

**What this supports.** Working technology is compatible with a speculative prop. Functionality is not the deciding criterion.

**Our use and its limit.** A can generate actual images. B can translate real messages. After the activity, these outputs can help visitors discuss an explicitly imagined future. A working feature alone does not supply that future question.

## Passage 5 · Leave space for people’s own interpretations



> Prefigurative futures (or pre-enactments?) also use props to transport viewers’ imagination but, whereas in this role they are bringing a specific future to the present so that it can be partially experienced in advance, user-tested, or prepared for, we are more interested in using props to transport viewers’ imagination into a thought experiment, or what-if, and allow enough room for them to make their own interpretations.

Chapter 6, pp. 93.

**What this supports.** Participants can give their own answers to an imagined situation. The researcher should leave those answers open.

**Our use and its limit.** A allows visitors to keep, adjust or stop a visual response. B allows people to understand each other and still disagree. Participants can reject either imagined museum rule.

## Passage 6 · People may make their own judgments



> We view people as free agents, not necessarily rational, but free to make up their own minds.

Chapter 9, pp. 161.

**What this supports.** This is a commitment to participants’ freedom to judge and choose.

**Our use and its limit.** People can decline AI, reject a result and choose a plain take-away. Email is optional. The freedom to choose is a design aim. It does not prove that every person can use the activity.

## Passage 7 · Create a space for discussion



> This form of design thrives on imagination and aims to open up new perspectives on what are sometimes called wicked problems, to create spaces for discussion and debate about alternative ways of being, and to inspire and encourage people’s imaginations to flow freely.

Chapter 1, pp. 2.

**What this supports.** Speculation opens a discussion about how life could be different. A feature test needs this wider question to become part of that discussion.

**Our use and its limit.** A discusses whether a changing digital version remains the same work. B discusses which words a museum should show. Both leave room for visitors to suggest a different future.

## Passage 8 · An alternative must open a gap with the present



> It is the gap between reality as we know it and the different idea of reality referred to in the critical design proposal that creates the space for discussion.

Chapter 3, pp. 35.

**What this supports.** A proposal opens discussion by making another arrangement available. Improving a feature within an unchanged arrangement is not sufficient on its own.

**Our use and its limit.** For each probe, identify the present default, the changed rule, the prop that makes it visible and the choice left to participants. Use this to explain selection and revision, not to score the probes with a universal scale.

## Passage 9 · The Statistical Clock · reasoning for Probe 01

Dunne & Raby with Michael Anastassiades. The Statistical Clock is an imagined household clock that reports deaths in different kinds of transport. It takes information people may avoid and puts it into an ordinary daily object. The designers ask what kind of life would make someone want this clock. The clock helps people discuss that life.

> What would have to change for a need like this to emerge?

Chapter 3, pp. 35–36.

**What this supports.** We borrow the clock’s way of making an everyday rule noticeable. In Probe 1, a small display record could show what changes when AI always supplies the first explanation. The subject changes from daily reminders of death to the starting point of a museum visit.

**Our use and its limit.** After the normal question task, show a clearly fictional display record marked “AI explanation shown first”. Ask what this helps, what it makes harder, and whether the visitor would keep or change the rule. Keep this as a short introduction. A separate study could examine AI going first. The first A and B trials already have more focused tasks. The clock gives us a way to ask about a daily rule. It does not show that putting AI first is always harmful.

## Passage 10 · The Islanders · reasoning for Probe 02

Charles Avery, discussed by Dunne & Raby. In Charles Avery’s The Islanders, drawings, objects, models and found bones appear to come from a traveller’s visit to an invented island. Viewers use these small clues to imagine a larger place and its way of life. A small physical thing can therefore help someone think about a world they cannot directly visit.

> It is the emphasis on transporting the imagination that distinguishes them from other object types including products, prototypes, and models.

Chapter 6, pp. 92–93.

**What this supports.** The book shows how a small thing can suggest a larger world. New A uses the visitor’s two actual images and a fictional museum label to make a future exhibition imaginable. We also use Plant Fiction’s clear marking of an imagined version. Neither case says that ordinary fabric becomes an original heritage object.

**Our use and its limit.** First complete the two-round fabric and AI activity. Then place its two results beside that clearly fictional label. Ask whether these should be called the same work, visitor responses, or separate new creations. Participants can propose another answer. Develop the new A around material action and visual response. Keep B around people exchanging meaning. Test whether visitors can connect a particular movement and image change to something they value in the source. The Islanders helps us design a small prop for imagining a museum. It does not prove that this future display or our new A will work.

## Passage 11 · Plant Fiction · reasoning for Probe 03

Troika, discussed by Dunne & Raby. Troika’s Plant Fiction presents invented plants with plausible images and short texts about how they might be used in a near-future London. The plants belong to a made-up situation. The project tells viewers that it is fiction, so they can explore the situation without being told a false discovery has occurred. One example, Selfeater, is imagined to break down its own material to help make fuel. Its picture and short story let viewers think about that future use.

> There is a very important difference between inviting viewers to “make-believe” and asking them to “believe.”

Chapter 6, pp. 94–95.

**What this supports.** Like the plant images and their short descriptions, a changed heritage image needs a clear situation. A possible version could show a pattern adapted for a stated present-day use. The original and the invented adaptation must be easy to tell apart.

**Our use and its limit.** Show the source beside one clearly labelled imagined adaptation. Give the adaptation a specific use. Ask which detail should remain and why. Keep this short, with no complex editing tools. Pause Probe 3 as a separate activity. New A already examines visual changes, with physical action guiding two AI responses. A separate image editor would add similar work unless it has a different, clear purpose. The plant case helps us explain an imagined version. It cannot provide historical evidence for a changed pattern.

## Passage 12 · United Micro Kingdoms · reasoning for Probe 04

Dunne & Raby. United Micro Kingdoms imagines several different societies and designs a vehicle for each one. Each vehicle shows choices about how people travel and live together. Looking at them helps viewers compare what each society makes easier and what it gives up. The pictured Digicars belong to a made-up society where software manages travel and road prices. The Biocar belongs to a society built around biotechnology. These two images show how different choices can shape everyday travel.

> Each vehicle would embody different ideologies, values, priorities, and belief systems—essentially alternative worldviews.

Chapter 9, pp. 173–174.

**What this supports.** The book makes different social choices visible through vehicles. B can make a museum’s choice visible through a display card: does the next visitor see the original words, an AI translation, or both? This is a choice about what people get to read.

**Our use and its limit.** After a real exchange, put a clearly fictional display-rule strip beside the card. Let participants keep that rule, ask for both versions, or suggest another rule. The real study continues to show the originals and translations together. Merge 4 with 5 as B. Probe 4 supplies the question about a shared display. Probe 5 adds a real reply that shows how another person understood it. We borrow a way of comparing choices. We do not sort Chinese and British visitors into the book’s invented societies.

## Passage 13 · Huggable Atomic Mushrooms · reasoning for Probe 05

Dunne & Raby with Michael Anastassiades. Huggable Atomic Mushrooms are soft objects shaped like clouds from nuclear tests. Their friendly feel sits beside a frightening subject. This makes viewers ask why an inviting object presents that subject in this way. Their meaning comes from this relationship between form and subject.

> It is through its demeanor that one starts to wonder just how serious it is.

Chapter 3, pp. 40–42.

**What this supports.** We borrow the question about how a friendly form changes a subject. In B, an attractive postcard might make an exchange easier to remember, but its picture might also change a serious, uncertain or personal description. We can compare the person’s approved words with the image they accept or reject.

**Our use and its limit.** Complete the real reply and clarification first. Then preview a postcard with the source credit, approved words and a separately labelled AI illustration. Ask whether the picture fits. For the future-display discussion, ask whether that version could speak for the person on a museum wall. Merge with 4 as B. Add the optional postcard after the exchange. Offer a plain card and optional email delivery. Keep AI emoji as a later idea so the first trial stays simple. This comparison does not require shocking images. It does not show that emojis have the same meaning for everyone. The postcard is not proof that two people understood each other.
