# Five-probe evaluation and initial selection

One continuous table links the activity, question, theory, speculation, feedback and decision. These are design judgments, not trial results.

| Probe and activity | Research question and HCI issue | How theory enters | Where speculation enters | Feedback and broad participation | Decision and evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **01 · Ask About a Detail**Select an object detail, write an initial interpretation, ask AI, then explain again.<br>**Input:** a detail, question and personal interpretation.<br> | **What do visitors rely on when judging an object’s authenticity, and how do those grounds change after AI responds?**HCI: interpretive authority, visible sources, and when visitors check or reject an explanation.<br> | Relational authenticity examines connections with objects, histories and personal experience. Iconicity and indexicality distinguish resemblance from an evidenced connection. A credible answer is not automatically an authentic experience. [1] [2] | **Present gap.** The current task offers AI answers, but no different museum arrangement. Checking accuracy alone investigates information use rather than a speculative alternative.**Book case.** The Statistical Clock: The clock in Do You Want to Replace the Existing Normal? reports fatalities by transport category. The authors imagine demand for an everyday object that reminds people of mortality. The question concerns the values and needs of that world, not a novel clock display.<br>What would have to change for a need like this to emerge?Dunne & Raby, pp. 35–36. [21] Passage and case context →<br>**Different arrangement.** An imagined museum gives the AI’s first interpretation default visibility. A visitor’s alternative can challenge it, but must be deliberately retained.<br>**Minimal prop and question.** After the ordinary task, show a clearly fictional first-interpretation record with the AI version marked as the default. Ask what a museum would have to value for this rule to make sense and whether the visitor would keep it.<br>**Trade-off.** Convenient orientation versus the visitor’s freedom to set the interpretive starting point.<br>**Effect on selection.** Keep 1 as an introduction. An authority-focused revision is possible, but it adds a separate question and is not needed for the first A/B trial.<br> | Feedback: rather ordinary. Low entry burden, although free questions can still be difficult without prior knowledge. Offer a detail choice and a short prompt. Allow participation without AI. | **Retain as an introduction.**AI question-and-answer deployments already exist. Studying the visitor’s grounds for judgment remains possible, but the interaction form offers limited novelty.<br>[13] [23] |
| **02 · Make a Shape**Respond to a detail using ordinary cloth or paper, describe making, review AI’s account and correct it.<br>**Input:** an observation, a material action and a first-person description.<br> | **How do visitors form or revise a sense of authenticity through hands-on making and AI interpretation?**HCI: the difference between a visual account and a lived experience, and who confirms the description.<br> | Relational authenticity traces connections between the visitor, making and the heritage object. Authenticity in AI co-writing helps examine whether an AI-assisted record still expresses the person’s experience. [1] [3] | **Present gap.** Handling cloth, using AI and printing a card are still ordinary activity features. None by itself changes the arrangement through which a visit is represented.**Book case.** The Islanders: The book describes drawings, objects, models and found bones attributed to a fictional traveller’s observations of an imaginary island. A small object invites viewers to imagine the wider world it belongs to. It is not simply an unusual object to own.<br>It is the emphasis on transporting the imagination that distinguishes them from other object types including products, prototypes, and models.Dunne & Raby, pp. 92–93. [21] Passage and case context →<br>**Different arrangement.** An imagined museum treats the AI-written card as the default lasting record of the visitor’s making. The visitor must decide whose account should represent that experience.<br>**Minimal prop and question.** Use the existing card, clearly labelled as a future-scenario prop, and ask which account would remain if only one were carried forward. The visitor may keep AI wording, replace it or reject the rule.<br>**Trade-off.** An orderly institutional record versus first-person authority over lived experience.<br>**Effect on selection.** Prioritize A conditionally. The same card already produced by the activity can make a broader recording rule discussable with little extra effort. The speculative question concerns representation, not whether the cloth becomes historical.<br> | Supervisor: 2 is closer to authenticity and should be broadly usable. Feedback: offer something to take away. No sewing skill is required. Visitors may arrange, point or direct assistance, and decline AI. | **Develop as Direction A.**The process can produce explanations and corrections. Cloth plus AI and printed souvenirs already have precedents. The candidate contribution concerns how visitors authorize or revise an AI account of their own making.<br>[3] [6] [7] [12] [15] [22] |
| **03 · Change One Detail**Circle a region, state what may change and what must remain, then compare an AI variant with the original.<br>**Input:** a selection, constraints and reasons for accepting or rejecting changes.<br> | **Which changes retain a meaningful connection with an object, and which break it for the visitor?**HCI: control over generation, preservation constraints and creative attribution.<br> | Distinguish “I like it”, “it looks similar” and “it is still connected to the original”. Aesthetic preference alone does not answer an authenticity question. [2] [5] | **Present gap.** Changing a colour or outline supplies a visual alternative, but does not yet identify which heritage value or interpretive arrangement is at stake.**Book case.** Plant Fiction: Troika’s fictitious plants combine plausible-looking images with short accounts of their roles in a near-future London. The project signals fiction while making a specific environmental arrangement imaginable. It is not presented as a factual plant discovery.<br>There is a very important difference between inviting viewers to “make-believe” and asking them to “believe.”Dunne & Raby, pp. 94–95. [21] Passage and case context →<br>**Different arrangement.** A possible later version imagines a museum that adapts a pattern according to a stated contemporary priority. A short fictional label identifies that priority and what the adaptation leaves behind.<br>**Minimal prop and question.** Compare the approved source and one clearly labelled imagined adaptation. Ask what should remain recognisable and why. Keep historical facts separate, and do not describe the variant as a restoration.<br>**Trade-off.** Contemporary reuse versus continuity with a source, its context and its meaning.<br>**Effect on selection.** Pause the standalone probe. First specify a meaningful adaptation and its contextual stakes. More attractive editing or a printed card does not resolve this gap.<br> | Feedback: the purpose and participant benefit are unclear, beyond enjoyable editing. Clothing interaction could become a later variant. Prompting and image editing may add unnecessary effort. | **Pause as a standalone probe.**Revisit only with a concrete interpretive task. Image transformation and extension already have precedents. Adding a printed card does not resolve the research gap.<br>[5] [14] [24] |
| **04 · Write a Label Together**Two people each write a line, AI combines them, then both revise the result or retain different versions.<br>**Input:** original wording, discussion, edits and reasons.<br> | **When AI organizes different interpretations, how do visitors decide whose meaning is retained and which differences should remain?**HCI: voice in a shared text and the possibility of artificial consensus.<br> | Authentication examines how an interpretation gains recognition. AI co-writing adds attention to personal voice and whether the shared wording is endorsed. [3] [17] | **Present gap.** Two languages or two opinions are not yet a different institutional arrangement. A jointly written label may even hide the disagreement the probe needs to investigate.**Book case.** United Micro Kingdoms: Different vehicles make alternative arrangements of everyday life and their trade-offs available for comparison. The comparison works because the objects embody different priorities. Four unusual vehicle designs alone would not communicate that argument.<br>Each vehicle would embody different ideologies, values, priorities, and belief systems—essentially alternative worldviews.Dunne & Raby, pp. 173–174. [21] Passage and case context →<br>**Different arrangement.** After a real exchange, imagine that a museum shows the AI translation as the public default while filing the originals separately. Visitors consider who may authorize that visibility rule.<br>**Minimal prop and question.** Use one existing conversation card plus a fictional display-rule strip. Participants can keep the default, require original and translation together, or refuse the proposed rule. These are discussion choices, not implemented publishing permissions.<br>**Trade-off.** Cross-language readability versus control over which voices and differences remain visible.<br>**Effect on selection.** Merge with 5 as B. 4 supplies the public interpretation question, while 5 adds a real recipient and a chance to correct understanding. A stated visibility rule gives the merged activity a more concrete speculative focus.<br> | Supervisor: accessible or community-friendly, with a stronger cross-cultural emphasis. Feedback: resembles community content and overlaps with 5. Remove the need for two people to arrive together and co-write synchronously. | **Merge with 5 as Direction B.**Follow a real expression through translation, reception and clarification around the same object. AI curation and interpersonal museum communication are precedents, rather than proof of this design’s effect.<br>[8] [9] [25] [26] |
| **05 · Share a Detail**Send an object-related message, review AI’s rewording, receive a reply and reflect on what was understood.<br>**Input:** an original message, chosen wording, reply and clarification.<br> | **After AI rewords a message, how do sender and recipient judge whether its meaning and personal expression remain?**HCI: expression control, recipient understanding and repair during communication.<br> | Relational authenticity examines how an object connects people. Personal voice concerns whether the author recognizes their meaning in the output. Fluency is not equivalent to authenticity. [1] [3] [16] | **Present gap.** Gifting or making a message warmer is not automatically speculative. Optional emoji can make an exchange pleasant without raising a question about what that representation retains or changes.**Book case.** Huggable Atomic Mushrooms: The soft, huggable mushroom forms refer to nuclear tests. The book describes a tension between their inviting material presence and what they represent. Their critical force is not equivalent to being cute, soft or amusing.<br>It is through its demeanor that one starts to wonder just how serious it is.Dunne & Raby, pp. 40–42. [21] Passage and case context →<br>**Different arrangement.** Within B’s imagined museum arrangement, an AI bridge message may make another person’s account easier or friendlier to read. Ask who can authorize that reframing when it changes hesitation, tone or a difficult personal association.<br>**Minimal prop and question.** Keep the original wording, AI wording and a real recipient’s reply on the same card. Discuss a meaningful difference and whether a future museum may retain the reworded version as the default. Do not require a disagreement or introduce a misleading historical claim.<br>**Trade-off.** Ease and warmth of communication versus fidelity to the sender’s tone and the object’s context.<br>**Effect on selection.** Merge with 4. The real recipient makes a representation’s consequence discussable. Keep generated expressions as a later branch, rather than treating visual play as the source of speculation.<br> | Feedback: combine with 4 and consider optional AI-generated expressions. Requiring a friend narrows participation. B lets a person respond to another real visitor without knowing them. Emoji are a later option, not a universal language. [18] | **Merge with 4 as Direction B.**Asynchronous exchange may broaden participation, but this remains to be tested. Follow both the sender’s intention and the recipient’s actual understanding.<br>[3] [9] [10] [26] |
